### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental design conflict: memtables are fast in-memory writes, but to make them durable and queryable they must be flushed to disk as SSTables, then continuously merged at deeper levels. Every write to an LSM-tree is written at least three times — memtable flush, level-0 to level-1 compaction, and eventually level-1 to level-2 compaction. The actual multiplier depends on the size ratio between levels (typically 10x), the number of levels, and the compaction strategy.

In practice, write amplification factors range from 10x to 50x for tiered compaction, and 3x to 10x for leveled compaction like RocksDB's default. The cost is real: wasted write endurance on SSDs (a consumer NVMe rated for 300 TBW starts degrading after roughly 30 TB of *logical* writes amplified 10x), degraded tail latency during compaction spikes (RocksDB's "stall" mechanism pauses writes when compaction falls behind), and unpredictable I/O patterns that make capacity planning difficult. The tradeoff is justified by the read performance — leveled compaction keeps reads at 1-2 IOPS per lookup — so you're spending write budget on read efficiency. The right compaction strategy depends entirely on your workload's read-to-write ratio.

You're looking at this in context of something specific — the DB layer for Clara's persistent memory, or the time-series ingestion for your trading data?

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC works by giving each reader a snapshot of the database as of the moment their transaction began. Writes create new row versions rather than overwriting existing ones, so the reader sees the old version undisturbed. No lock is taken on the read — the writer is free to commit a new version in parallel. The reader only needs to know which row versions are "visible" to them, determined by comparing each version's creation/expiration transaction IDs against their own snapshot.

The garbage is **dead row versions** — versions that are no longer visible to any active transaction. Two kinds:

1. **Expired versions** — superseded by a newer version from a committed write, and no running snapshot sees them anymore.
2. **Aborted versions** — created by a transaction that later rolled back, never visible to anyone.

These accumulate in the table's storage pages (heap or index pages), bloating disk and memory. PostgreSQL calls the cleanup **VACUUM** — it reclaims space from dead tuples. InnoDB does it via the **purge thread**, which walks the history list and removes versions whose transaction IDs are older than the oldest active snapshot. If vacuuming lags, you get table bloat and transaction ID wraparound — the latter is a hard stop that forces the database offline until cleaned.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and domain logic.

The main risks, in order of how often they actually cause projects to fail:

1. **Network as the new primitive** — What was a local function call is now an RPC over an unreliable, latent network. Developers treat it synchronously, and the system cascades failures instead of degrading gracefully.

2. **Distributed data consistency** — ACID transactions across services are gone. Teams reach for eventual consistency but don't design for the window of inconsistency, or they cargo-cult sagas without the compensating transaction logic that makes them safe.

3. **The bounded-context trap** — Teams split services by technical layer (one service for auth, one for DB access) instead of by business capability. This gives you distributed monolith — all the pain of network calls with none of the independence benefit.

4. **Testing complexity** — Unit tests still work. Integration tests now require standing up N dependent services, mocking their state, and managing test data across boundaries. Most teams shrink from this and ship untested interactions.

5. **Observability debt** — Monoliths get by with logs and a debugger. Distributed systems require structured logging with trace IDs, metrics aggregation, and distributed tracing — all built in from day one, not bolted on after the first incident.

6. **Team-architecture mismatch** — Conway's Law works both ways. If you split the monolith but keep one team owning everything, you've added operational complexity for no organizational gain. The architecture only delivers when each service has a team with the autonomy to deploy independently.

The common thread: the technical risks are solvable. The organizational ones (team structure, release coordination, ownership boundaries) are what stall migrations.
