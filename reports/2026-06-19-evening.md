### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental design tension: they trade read performance and space for sustained write throughput, but every write gets rewritten multiple times before it settles.

The sources are threefold. First, **compaction**: when the tree merges SSTables from level N into level N+1, every piece of data in the overlapping key range is read, sorted, and rewritten — even if only a single new entry triggered the merge. A key inserted at L0 might be compacted into L1, L2, and L3 before it reaches its final level; each compaction is a full rewrite of that key. Second, **the write-ahead log**: every mutation is written to the WAL before it touches the memtable, even though that log is never read during normal operation — it exists only for crash recovery. That's a full synchronous write per mutation that has zero query benefit. Third, **the memtable flush**: when a memtable fills and is frozen, its entire contents are written to disk as an L0 SSTable, even though many of those entries will be compacted again moments later.

In practice the costs are measurable and steep. Disk bandwidth becomes the bottleneck, not CPU — SSDs have finite write endurance measured in terabytes written (TBW), and high write amplification directly shortens drive lifespan. A workload with 10x write amplification burns through an SSD's rated life ten times faster than a B-tree performing the same number of logical writes. Latency spikes follow each compaction cycle, particularly in leveled compaction where the largest levels trigger full-merge passes that can saturate disk I/O for seconds. Size-tiered compaction reduces amplification at the cost of higher space overhead, but even then the amplification floor is roughly 4–6x for update-heavy workloads.

The tradeoff LSM-trees accept is this: write amplification buys compaction — and compaction buys sequential I/O patterns and high compression ratios. Whether that's worth it depends on whether your workload is write-dominated and whether your hardware budget includes premium SSD endurance.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC works by keeping a separate snapshot of each row at the point-in-time a read transaction begins, rather than using read locks. When a writer updates a row, it doesn't overwrite the existing one — it creates a new version tagged with the writer's transaction ID. The reader sees only the version whose transaction committed before the reader's snapshot started, and never sees the uncommitted or newer version. The writer proceeds in parallel; the reader is never blocked because there's always a stable version to return.

The garbage is precisely those row versions that are no longer visible to any active or future transaction — versions created by committed write transactions that have been superseded by a newer version. This accumulates as dead tuples (in PostgreSQL terms) or version chains that must be reclaimed by a vacuum or compaction process. The cleanup cost is bounded by write rate and snapshot duration: long-running read transactions delay reclamation because they still need the older versions to satisfy snapshot isolation.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability.

The main risks when splitting a monolith are:

**Network as a new failure domain** — what were in-process function calls become remote calls that can time out, retry-storm, or silently fail. The system now has partial failure as a first-class concern: any service can be down while others are healthy, and you need circuit breakers, timeouts, and bulkheads where none existed before.

**Distributed data consistency** — a single database transaction that updated three tables in the monolith now spans three services and three databases. You lose ACID guarantees and must choose between eventual consistency with saga patterns or two-phase commit with its own availability trade-offs. Most teams underestimate how much business logic was implicitly correct because of serializable transactions.

**Latency accumulation** — a request that took 50ms inside a single process now fans out across 5-15 services, each adding network latency, serialization overhead, and queue depth. A naive synchronous chain of 10 services at 20ms each is 200ms before any logic runs. You can't fix this with caching alone — you have to redesign the call topology.

**Boundary mistakes are the most expensive error** — if you split on technical layers (one service for all reads, one for all writes) instead of business domains, every feature change touches multiple services. The migration becomes a frozen distributed monolith: worse latency, same coupling. Getting the bounded context wrong costs months to unwind because data has already been repartitioned.

**Observability debt** — in a monolith, a single log file and a profiler tell you what happened. After splitting, you need distributed tracing, structured logging across 20 services, metrics aggregation, and correlation IDs threaded through every hop. Most teams build this after the first outage they can't debug.

**The strangler fig pattern done wrong** — extracting one service at a time while the monolith still owns shared tables is the safe approach, but teams commonly extract services without decoupling the schema first. The new service and the monolith both write to the same database table, creating write conflicts and coupling that defeats the purpose of the split. Data must be owned and sealed before the service boundary is drawn.
