### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the tension between immutability and compaction. New data is written as fresh SSTables in Level 0, but reads would become catastrophically slow without periodically merging and re-sorting those files into higher levels. Each compaction pass reads a set of SSTables and writes new ones, then discards the originals — so the same key-value pair is written to disk multiple times over its lifetime despite only being inserted once from the application's perspective.

The cost has three distinct dimensions:

- **Write amplification factor itself**: For LevelDB's default size ratio of 10 (each level is ~10x larger than the previous), a key that lives through all levels is re-written roughly 10–50 times by the time it reaches the final level, depending on how many levels it survives and how compaction overlaps. Higher ratios reduce amplification but increase read cost (more files to merge per read). Lower ratios smooth out write bursts but inflate the factor further.

- **Bandwidth and I/O tail latency**: On HDDs, the rotational seek cost of random compaction reads dominates — the drive spends most of its time positioning heads, not transferring data. On SSDs, write amplification directly consumes write endurance; a TLC NAND drive rated for ~3,000 P/E cycles sees its effective lifespan reduced by the same factor. This is why high-throughput LSM workloads (time-series, log ingestion) burn through consumer SSDs in months.

- **Space amplification**: Compaction is never instantaneous — old SSTables persist until the merge completes, so physical disk usage spikes during compaction. RocksDB's universal compaction (used in Cassandra, MyRocks) mitigates this by merging all levels simultaneously, but at the cost of higher peak write amp. Typical steady-state space overhead is 10–50% depending on compaction strategy.

The real-world cost surfaces under write-heavy workloads: compaction falls behind, and the system enters a "write stall" where it pauses incoming writes to let compaction catch up. This is Kafka's trade-off — its LSM-adjacent log architecture segments aggressively to limit compaction scope, keeping write amp closer to 1.5–2x at the cost of more files and slightly slower restart times. In contrast, Cassandra's default size-tiered compaction can hit 10–20x write amp under sustained load, which is why production deployments commonly switch to leveled or time-window compaction strategies.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each reader a consistent **snapshot** of the database as of the transaction's start time, using versioned rows (each row carries a `created_by` and `deleted_by` transaction ID). A writer's insert/update/delete creates a *new* version of the row — the reader's snapshot still references the *old* version, so neither one blocks waiting for a lock. The reader finishes its work on the old version; the writer's new version is invisible to it until the reader's transaction ends.

The garbage is **dead row versions** — row versions no longer visible to any active or future transaction (i.e., no snapshot can still see them). These pile up in the table's page file as outdated tuples that must be reclaimed by a **VACUUM**-style garbage collector (PostgreSQL's autovacuum, MySQL InnoDB's purge thread), or by a database restart that scans and rebuilds. The accumulation can cause table bloat and degraded index scan performance if the cleaner doesn't keep up.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and domain logic. The goal is to enable independent scaling, faster deployments per team, and technology heterogeneity.

The main risks:

**Network as the new source of truth.** A monolith calls functions in-process — reliable, zero-latency, no partial failure. Microservices replace those calls with network hops. Latency, timeouts, retries, and cascading failures enter the system. You go from handling crashes to handling silence, which is harder to detect and diagnose.

**Data consistency breaks.** A monolith uses a single ACID transaction across bounded contexts. Split them, and you lose that. You now need sagas, eventual consistency, or distributed transactions — each with their own failure modes. The team that doesn't plan for what happens when step 3 of a 5-step saga fails halfway through will find out the hard way.

**The bounded context discovery problem.** Microservices are only as good as their boundaries. Get the boundary wrong — sharing a database, forcing synchronous calls for what should be async, splitting what should be a single service because two teams wanted autonomy — and you get a distributed monolith. All the cost, none of the benefit, harder to refactor.

**Operational complexity.** One deployable becomes N. Each has its own CI/CD, health checks, logging, monitoring, observability. The team that thinks "we'll figure out the monitoring later" will be debugging production issues with no tracing context, no aggregated logs, and no way to tell which service caused the timeout. Martin Kleppmann covers this exact trap in *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* — the system becomes harder to reason about by an order of magnitude.

**Team coordination tax.** Independent services require aligned contracts, API versioning, and backward compatibility. One team changing a payload format without notifying consumers is a production incident waiting to happen. The coordination doesn't disappear — it shifts from code-level to interface-level, and that is not inherently cheaper.
