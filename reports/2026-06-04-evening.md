### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between horizontal and vertical scaling. What are the tradeoffs of each? Answer from your training knowledge.

Horizontal scaling adds more machines to distribute load across a cluster — each handles a fraction of the work, and failure of any single node doesn't take the system down. Vertical scaling gives the existing machine more resources — bigger CPU, more RAM, faster disk — which keeps the architecture simple but caps at the hardware ceiling of one box.

The tradeoffs are opposite sides of the same coin.
- **Horizontal**: Higher operational complexity — you need load balancers, service discovery, data consistency across nodes, and network latency management. But it's effectively unlimited in scale and provides fault tolerance by design. The cost curve is linear: ten machines cost ten times one machine.
- **Vertical**: Simple to implement — same code, same database, same config. No distributed systems problems to solve. But you hit a hard ceiling (the largest instance your cloud provider offers or the physical limits of your hardware), and failure is catastrophic: one box goes down, the entire service goes with it. The cost curve is super-linear: the jump from 64GB to 1TB RAM costs far more than 16x the price of 64GB.

The decision rule: if you can predict your peak load within a factor of 2, vertical is simpler and cheaper. If you're growing unpredictably or need availability guarantees, horizontal is the only real option.

### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between a primary key and a foreign key in relational databases. Answer from your training knowledge.

Primary key uniquely identifies each row in a table — one per table, must be unique and non-null. Foreign key is a column (or set) in one table that references the primary key of another table, enforcing referential integrity across the relationship. The primary key is the identity of the row in its own table; the foreign key is the link that says "this row belongs to that row over there."

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each reader a snapshot of the database as of the moment the transaction began. Writes create new row versions instead of overwriting existing ones, so the reader sees the old version in its snapshot, and the writer proceeds on the new version — no lock contention between them because neither waits on the other. The garbage is **dead row versions** (old tuples that are no longer visible to any active transaction). Two forms: **expired versions** that no snapshot references, and **bloat** — the accumulation of these dead tuples occupying pages that have to be vacuumed or compacted before the space can be reused. PostgreSQL calls this the dead-tuple pileup that `VACUUM` reclaims.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a single, self-contained application (the monolith) into multiple independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and responsible for a specific business capability. You don't "split" code — you extract bounded contexts, one at a time.

The main risks, in order of how often they actually kill projects:

1. **Premature decomposition** — You extract services before you understand what the bounded contexts actually are, ending up with a distributed monolith that has all the latency of a network call and none of the isolation benefits. The cost of a wrong service boundary is paid forever.

2. **Distributed transactions** — What was a single ACID transaction in the monolith now spans multiple services. Teams either paper over it with eventual consistency patterns they don't fully understand (Sagas, outbox), or they try to force two-phase commit across network boundaries and discover the availability cost.

3. **Network as a new failure domain** — Latency, timeouts, partial failures, retry storms, cascading failures from a single degraded service. The monolith either worked or it didn't; a microservices system can be in 47 degraded states and you need observability to tell which one.

4. **Data coupling masquerading as service boundaries** — Services that share a database, or services that need constant synchronous queries into each other's data, are not actually independent. Every cross-service join or foreign key reference in a separate database is a coupling point that defeats the purpose.

5. **Testing complexity explosion** — Unit testing a service is fine. Integration testing N services with their real dependencies, in realistic network conditions, with controllable failure injection — that's an entirely different engineering discipline that most teams don't budget for.

6. **Operational surface area** — One deployable becomes ten. You now need container orchestration, service discovery, API gateways, circuit breakers, distributed tracing, centralized logging, and someone on-call who understands all of it. The monolith needed a process manager and a restart script.

7. **Team coordination overhead** — Services owned by separate teams that must coordinate on API contracts, versioning, and breaking changes creates organizational friction that the monolith's single codebase never had. Conway's law inverts: you changed the architecture, but the org chart still has the same communication paths.

The migration itself fails most often from trying to do too much at once — a big-bang rewrite. The only pattern that survives contact with reality is Strangler Fig: carve one service at a time, route traffic incrementally, keep the monolith running until the extracted service is proven in production, then delete the old code and move to the next extraction.
