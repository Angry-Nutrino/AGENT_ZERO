### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental tension between their design—immutable, append-only writes—and the compaction process needed to keep reads fast.

**Where it comes from — three distinct sources:**

First, **flush from the memtable to an SSTable on disk.** Every write hits the in-memory balanced tree (memtable), and when it fills, that entire structure is serialized to disk as an immutable SSTable. That's one write of the data.

Second, **compaction** — the cascading merge of multiple SSTable tiers. An LSM-tree typically uses leveled or size-tiered compaction. In leveled compaction (RocksDB's default, Cassandra's LCS), a write from level 0 is merged into level 1 against all overlapping SSTables. That rewrite of multiple files compounds. Write amplification per level is roughly `(num_files_merged + 1) / num_files_merged` — but across all levels, it accumulates. For a 10-level tree with 10x size ratios, the total write amplification is often 10–50x the original data size.

Third, **the process itself creates SSTables of the compaction output** that themselves get compacted in the next level. Each byte that enters the system is rewritten multiple times before it settles into the largest tier.

**What it costs in practice:**

Disk endurance on SSDs is finite — a consumer TLC NAND drive typically handles 300–600 TBW (TeraBytes Written). A write amplification of 10x on a database that ingests 100 GB/day means 1 TB/day of actual disk writes. That drive fails in under a year. In datacenter deployments, this is the primary reason RocksDB or Cassandra nodes burn through SSDs faster than expected.

Latency spikes — compaction runs consume I/O bandwidth and CPU. A background compaction of a 100 MB SSTable against four overlapping 100 MB SSTables means 500 MB read-then-merge-then-write, stalling foreground writes when the compaction queue backs up.

Space amplification — during compaction, both the source and destination SSTables exist simultaneously. You need headroom: RocksDB recommends keeping free space above 10% of total data, and if you hit space exhaustion during compaction, the engine stalls.

The tradeoff LSM-trees accept is simple: write amplification for write throughput. They absorb bursts at memtable speed and defer the cost to background compaction. Every LSM parameter—level size ratio, number of tiers, compaction concurrency, compression—is a lever trading write amplification against read amplification and space amplification. There is no free parameter.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC works by giving each transaction a snapshot of the database at a point in time — typically its start timestamp or a logical snapshot ID. A writer creates a new version of a row rather than overwriting the existing one. A reader that started earlier simply reads the old version from its snapshot, so the writer's exclusive lock (or its equivalent) covers only the version it's writing, never a row a reader might still need. The reader never blocks; the writer never blocks on a reader.

The garbage it creates is **dead row versions** — tuples that are no longer visible to any active transaction. Each update or delete leaves behind the prior version of the row. Over time these accumulate in the table's storage pages, degrading scan performance and wasting space. PostgreSQL calls this "bloat"; the cleanup process is VACUUM. Some databases (Oracle, InnoDB) use an undo log to keep the old versions externally, and purge them when the oldest active transaction predates them. Either way, the cost is deferred maintenance — if the system has long-running read transactions or a slow vacuum, the garbage pile grows faster than it's collected, and you get unbounded bloat.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and running in its own process.

The main risks, ranked by how often they derail teams:

1. **Network as a new primitive** — What was a local function call becomes an HTTP call or message. Latency goes from microseconds to milliseconds. Teams don't redesign for partial failure, so a cascading outage in one service takes down everything. Retries without backoff amplify the problem. Circuit breakers, timeouts, and bulkheads are not optional — they're structural requirements.

2. **Distributed transactions or their absence** — The monolith had ACID. Now a single business operation touches three databases. Teams reach for two-phase commit (which doesn't scale) or sagas (which require compensating transactions and eventual consistency tolerance). If the team hasn't internalized that they're trading consistency for availability, they'll ship data corruption.

3. **Data coupling masquerading as service boundaries** — Services share a database, or they cross-reference tables, or they do joins across service boundaries because it was easier. The monolith's schema wasn't bounded by domain — it was bounded by application logic. Extracting clean bounded contexts from a relational schema that was never designed for them is archaeology, not engineering. Most teams cut corners here and end up with a distributed monolith.

4. **Observability debt** — In a monolith, a single log file and a profiler told you what was happening. In microservices, a request fans out across 8 services. If you haven't invested in distributed tracing, structured logging, and metrics aggregation *before* the migration is underway, you're debugging blind. Teams discover this 3 months in when a production incident takes 4 hours to trace.

5. **Organizational Conway's Law mismatch** — Microservices don't fix team communication problems. If the monolith was built by one team and now you split into three teams owning three services, the interfaces between those services are a proxy for the conversations those teams aren't having. Service boundaries designed without organizational context will be wrong.

The one that kills projects most frequently is #3 — the data coupling. Everything else can be retrofitted. A database shared across services is a hard constraint that gets more expensive to untangle the longer it persists.
