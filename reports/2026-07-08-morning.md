### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control, and when each is the right choice. Answer from your training knowledge.

**Optimistic concurrency control (OCC)** assumes conflicts are rare. You read the data, do your work, and only at commit time do you check whether the version/row has changed since you read it. If it has, you abort and retry. No locks held during the read phase — just a validation step at the end.

**Pessimistic concurrency control (PCC)** assumes conflicts are likely enough that you can't risk a retry. You lock the data at read time (`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`) and hold that lock through your transaction. No one else touches it until you release it.

**Choose optimistic when:** contention is low, retries are cheap, and your transactions are short-lived enough that the validation step is quick. Good fit for read-heavy workloads, web apps where users mostly look at data, and scenarios where you can tolerate occasional aborts — because aborts are the exception, not the rule.

**Choose pessimistic when:** contention is high, the cost of retrying a conflicted transaction is unacceptable (long-running batch, complex multi-step op, payment), or you need strict serializability without the complexity of serializable snapshot isolation. Databases with row-level locks and short lock windows can handle this well. Also the right call when you know you'll write most things you read in the same transaction — why gamble if you're going to commit anyway.

**The real tradeoff isn't speed — it's retry cost vs lock overhead.** OCC burns cycles on aborts. PCC burns cycles on lock management and reduced concurrency. The point where they cross depends on your collision rate, transaction duration, and how expensive a rollback is in your domain.

### Q10 [CHAT] — What is the difference between a process and a thread, and when would you use multiprocessing vs threading in Python? Answer from your training knowledge.

Processes have isolated memory spaces, separate GILs, and communicate via IPC. Threads share the same memory space and synchronise via locks, but are GIL-bound in CPython for CPU work.

Use multiprocessing for CPU-bound work — image processing, numerical computation, any loop that saturates a core. Use threading for I/O-bound work — file reads, network calls, database queries where the thread yields the GIL during wait. For concurrent I/O across many connections, `asyncio` beats both. For mixed workloads — long-running compute with interspersed I/O — a process pool with an async coordinator is the pattern.

The pure-write locks (`self._write_locks`, a dict of `path → asyncio.Lock`) are never touched by `release_task` for two reasons:

1. **No task ownership.** Each lock is acquired and released within the scope of a single `acquire_write`/release pair. The caller's `try/finally` handles release (line 89 in the docstring). The lock is bound to a *call*, not to a *task*.

The only cost is the lock object's memory, which is negligible and bounded by the number of distinct paths ever written, not the number of tasks.

### Q15 [CHAT] — Explain tail latency (p99/p999): why it dominates user experience in distributed services more than average latency, and one architectural technique to tame it. Answer from your training knowledge.

Tail latency — p99/p999 — is the long tail of slow responses. In a distributed service, a single user request fans out across dozens of microservices. If each service has a p99 of 50ms, the *system-level* p99 isn't 50ms — it's 50ms × the number of sequential hops. Worse, if any one of those 50 parallel leaf calls hits its p999, the whole request waits. Average hides this: a 50ms average could mean 90% of requests finish in 5ms and 10% take 455ms — every 10th user feels a half-second stall. In practice, that 10% abandons the page, refreshes, or calls support. Average latency is an accounting figure. Tail latency is the user experience.

The canonical taming technique is **hedged requests** (Google's "Stochastic Probing" / "The Tail at Scale"). Instead of waiting for one backend node to respond, send the same request to two replicas in parallel. The first response wins; the second is cancelled. This transforms p99-to-service from "wait for the slowest node" to "use the fastest of two." At 1M+ QPS it requires careful resource accounting (you're doubling load at the leaves), but the payoff is dramatic: p999 can drop by an order of magnitude because the odds that both replicas are in their p999 simultaneously are p999².
