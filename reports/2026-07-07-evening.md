### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental tension between sequential write efficiency and the need to maintain sorted order across levels.

**Where it comes from:**

Three sources, layered on top of each other:

1. **Compaction.** The dominant source. When a level fills up, the LSM merges its sorted runs with the overlapping runs in the next level. Every key-value pair that survives compaction is read from the old file and written to the new one — even if the data hasn't changed. With leveled compaction (RocksDB's default), data in L1 might be rewritten 10–15 times as it moves down through levels. Tiered compaction is gentler on write amplification (~2–3x) but at the cost of more read amplification.

2. **Memtable flush.** The in-memory sorted structure (memtable) is flushed to disk as an SSTable. That's one write per insertion eventually. Negligible in isolation — every storage engine must write data at least once.

3. **WAL.** The write-ahead log writes each mutation before it touches the memtable. This is the same cost any crash-safe storage engine pays, not LSM-specific.

**What it costs in practice:**

- **Write endurance on SSDs.** Consumer SSDs are rated for 150–600 TBW (terabytes written). A RocksDB workload with 10x write amplification means every 100 GB of logical writes burns 1 TB of SSD endurance. For high-throughput ingestion pipelines — time-series, log aggregation — this is the bottleneck that determines hardware lifespan.

- **CPU cycles.** Compaction is not free. Deserializing, re-sorting, re-serializing, checksumming — it's pure overhead that doesn't serve reads. A busy LSM can spend 30–50% of its CPU budget on compaction threads. Your compaction strategy directly determines how much compute you have left for serving queries.

- **P99 latency jitter.** Compaction competes for I/O. When a large compaction job hits the same disk as foreground writes, write latency spikes. It's the classic LSM pain point: to smooth throughput you must throttle compaction, but throttling pushes the merge cost forward and eventually you pay worse.

- **Space amplification (the hidden tradeoff).** The whole point of write amplification is to reclaim space from deleted/overwritten entries. If you reduce compaction frequency (lower write amplification), space amplification rises — Tombstones pile up, obsolete versions accumulate, read amplification also rises. You cannot optimize both axes. This is the parameter space you tune: write_amplification = f(compaction_style, level_size_ratio, max_bytes_for_level_multiplier).

**The practical rule of thumb designers use:** Leveled compaction gives ~10–15x worst-case write amplification for predictable read latency. Tiered compaction gives ~2–3x write amplification but unpredictable read cost and worse space amplification. If you're on an NVMe with high endurance and you need consistent reads, level. If you're on constrained flash and your reads are cache-friendly, tier. There's no free lunch — LSM-trees trade write cost for write throughput, and compaction is the price of admission.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC works because readers never acquire locks — they see a *snapshot* of the database as of a specific transaction ID (or timestamp). When a writer begins updating a row, it creates a new version of that row, tagged with its transaction ID. Existing readers continue reading the old version using their snapshot. The writer's new version is invisible to them until the writer commits, and even then, only transactions that start after the commit see it. The result: a reader and writer can operate on the same row simultaneously without either waiting.

The garbage is *dead row versions* — old versions that are no longer visible to any active transaction. Every UPDATE creates one (the previous version becomes unreachable once all snapshots that could see it are closed). Every DELETE marks the row as deleted but leaves the version in place until it's safe to remove. These accumulate in the table and indexes, bloating storage, slowing sequential scans, and degrading index performance. PostgreSQL calls this cleanup **VACUUM**; MySQL/InnoDB runs it as a background purge thread. The bill always comes due — defer the cleanup and you pay in read amplification and bloat.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a single, unified application (a monolith) into multiple smaller, independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each responsible for a bounded domain.

The main risks are:

1. **Network becomes the new backplane** — In-monolith calls succeed or fail within a process. Cross-service calls introduce latency, partial failure, and the need for timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, and bulkheads. Most teams underestimate how much of their codebase implicitly relies on synchronous, reliable, in-process calls.

2. **Data consistency collapses** — The monolith's ACID transactions across tables become distributed operations with no single database to coordinate against. Teams end up needing sagas, outbox patterns, or eventual consistency — all of which are harder to reason about and test.

3. **Service boundaries are guessed, not designed** — The first cut of service boundaries tends to follow the monolith's module structure, which was shaped by the monolith's accidental complexity, not domain boundaries. The result is chatty services, distributed god classes across service boundaries, or split-transaction hell. Conway's law will punish you here — the service boundaries that survive are the ones your team's communication structure can sustain, not the ones your domain model wants.

4. **Operational surface area multiplies** — One deployable becomes N. That means N build pipelines, N health checks, N log streams, N tracing contexts, N config files, N container images, N potential failure modes. If your observability stack wasn't designed for distributed tracing from day one, you're debugging with HTTP status codes and prayer.

5. **The distributed join problem** — Any query that joined two tables in the monolith now requires an API call to a second service, or a denormalized cache/replica, or a CQRS split. None of these are free. All of them add latency and staleness.

6. **False speed** — Teams adopt microservices because they want independent deployability and team autonomy. But until you have the CI/CD, contract testing, feature flags, and backward-compatible API versioning to actually release independently, you've just traded deployment of one thing for coordinated deployments of many things. The bottleneck shifts from "takes too long to deploy the monolith" to "takes too long to coordinate the deploy of 12 services that all depend on each other."
