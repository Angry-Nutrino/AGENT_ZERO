### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification (WA) is the ratio of physical bytes written to storage divided by logical bytes written by the application. In LSM engines it typically sits at 5–30×, and it's the price of the design's core bet: convert random writes into sequential ones by buffering and rewriting.

**Where it comes from**

1. **WAL** — Every write first hits the write-ahead log. It's sequential and amortized by group commit, so it's cheap, but it's still one physical write per batch that a pure in-memory design wouldn't pay.
2. **Memtable flush** — When a memtable fills, it's written out as an SSTable. This is the only pass that writes your data honestly: once. Up to this point WA is roughly 2× (log + flush).
3. **Compaction** — The dominant term. Levels grow by a size ratio T (typically ~10×). Every time data moves from level i to i+1, overlapping SSTables are read back, merged, and the merged output is written again. Each byte that reaches the bottom level is rewritten roughly once per level it traverses. With 4–5 levels, a byte written once is physically written 5–6 times — before you even count L0, where files overlap heavily and a single key can live in several files, making every L0→L1 merge drag in extra data.
4. **Tombstones** — Deletes are markers that must propagate through every level before the old data can be discarded. Until then the dead data still participates in merges and occupies space.

So WA ≈ (levels × merge cost) + WAL overhead, which lands at 10–30× for LevelDB/RocksDB-style leveled compaction. Size-tiered compaction (Cassandra's default, older HBase) trades the other way: whole runs get merged without rewriting everything at each step, so WA drops to ~2–4× — at the cost of worse read amplification and space amplification.

**What it costs in practice**

- **Flash endurance.** Cells have finite program/erase cycles: QLC ~1–1.5k, TLC ~3–5k, enterprise MLC ~10k+. At 20× WA, a drive's rated TBW is consumed at 20× the logical write rate. This is the number one reason LSM engines chew through consumer SSDs under sustained load.
- **Throughput ceiling.** Device bandwidth is the hard limit. A drive with ~500 MB/s sequential write capacity only sustains ~25–50 MB/s of application writes at 10–20× WA, because the rest is rewrites. Worse: if compaction can't keep up, L0 grows, overlap grows, and WA climbs — a feedback loop that caps throughput or spikes it during catch-up.
- **Tail latency.** Logical writes return fast because they're buffered in the memtable. But compaction steals I/O and CPU in the background, so production workloads see periodic latency spikes. Reads that must probe multiple levels — especially with a deep L0 — pay extra seeks during those windows. The cost lands in your P99, not your average.
- **Space amplification tension.** You can reduce WA by raising the level ratio (fewer levels, fewer passes), but then obsolete and updated data lingers longer and reads probe fewer, larger SSTables. Write amp, read amp, space amp: every compaction policy is a point in that triangle, and there's no simultaneous optimum.
- **Observability.** It's directly measurable. RocksDB exposes per-level compaction stats; SSDs report total LBAs written via SMART. Comparing the drive's physical bytes written against the application's logical write count gives you the real-world WA figure, and a spike is usually a diagnostic signal — misconfigured flush triggers, L0 not draining, or a compaction policy mismatched to the ingest pattern.

The levers, if you're tuning one: compaction policy (leveled vs. tiered vs. hybrid), the level size ratio, memtable size and count before flush, and deferring compaction during bulk loads. This is the trade your DDIA reading covers — the LSM's bet is that sequential rewrites are cheap enough to pay for themselves via deferred, steady write ingestion. WA is the invoice.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC decouples reads from writes by versioning data instead of locking it.

**The mechanism.** When a transaction writes a row, it doesn't overwrite the old value — it creates a new version and keeps the old one around, tagged with metadata about which transaction created it and which one deleted/superseded it. Readers don't take locks at all. They take a snapshot — effectively a point in time defined by "which transactions had committed when I started" — and walk the version chain to find the version that was current at that moment. In PostgreSQL that's the xmin/xmax system columns and the visibility rules applied against the transaction ID horizon. In InnoDB the current version lives in the clustered index and older versions are reconstructed from the undo log. Either way, a reader racing a writer just sees the older committed version and continues — no contention, no blocking, both proceed.

This is the fundamental departure from strict two-phase locking, where a read lock and a write lock on the same row are mutually exclusive and someone has to wait.

**The garbage.** Every old version is a candidate for cleanup as soon as it can no longer be seen by *any* active transaction's snapshot. That condition is: the transaction that created it has committed or aborted, and every transaction that started before that commit has finished — so no reader can ever reach the old version again. Until then, it must stay, because a long-running transaction's snapshot may still legitimately see it.

That accumulation is the garbage MVCC creates, and it's concrete and physical:

- **PostgreSQL:** dead tuples — old row versions still sitting in table and index pages. Cleanup is `VACUUM` (autovacuum in the background), which marks space reusable, updates the visibility map, and keeps index bloat in check. If vacuum lags, tables and indexes bloat, and index-only scans degrade.
- **InnoDB:** old versions pinned in pages plus undo log records. The purge thread reclaims them. If purge lags — which happens when some transaction holds a snapshot open — the undo log's history list grows and the tablespace bloats. An idle-but-open transaction can pin gigabytes of garbage indefinitely.

So the recurring operational law of any MVCC system: **the cleanup horizon is set by the oldest still-open transaction.** Whoever holds a snapshot open the longest decides how much garbage accumulates. That's why "idle in transaction" is a famous Postgres problem, and why monitoring transaction age matters more in MVCC databases than in locking ones. The trade-off is explicit: you buy non-blocking reads with write amplification, version metadata overhead, and a continuous reclamation job that the database can never fully skip.

### The two `return_exceptions=True` calls — and why they must be exception-tolerant

Both are shutdown drains. Both must not let one failure abort the rest because they run **after an explicit `cancel()`** — and a cancelled asyncio task does not exit cleanly; it raises `CancelledError` inside the coroutine at its nearest `await` point. So while a normal gather would fail fast on the first exception and leave the remaining tasks un-awaited (and their exceptions un-retrieved → `"Task exception was never retrieved"` warnings, plus abandoned coroutines), with `return_exceptions=True` every task is guaranteed to be awaited to completion, exception swallowed per-task.

Concretely, per site:

- **`background_tasks.py:64` (Scheduler)** — `stop()` calls `t.cancel()` on every trigger-loop task, then gathers. Each loop is mid `asyncio.sleep()` when cancelled, so `CancelledError` in *every* task is nearly certain, not the exception. Without `return_exceptions=True`, the first `CancelledError` would propagate immediately, the remaining tasks would be abandoned — and the scheduler's `start()` re-creates task handles in `self._tasks`, so a stale handle from an un-awaited run could linger. The tolerance is what makes stop() reliably drain all loops before `self._tasks.clear()`.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a single deployable application — the monolith — into multiple independently deployable services, each owning its own code and data and communicating over a network via APIs or events. The critical part most people underweight: it is not primarily a code refactor. It is a transformation across four layers at once — deployment topology, data ownership, operational model, and team structure (Conway's law follows whether you planned it or not).

The main risks, roughly in order of how badly they hurt:

**1. Data consistency and transactions.** This is the hardest problem, and usually the one discovered last. ACID transactions inside a monolith span tables in one database; across services there is no equivalent. Distributed transactions (2PC) are a trap — they couple services for the sake of atomicity and reintroduce the coupling you were trying to escape. The discipline you need instead: sagas, the outbox pattern, and accepting eventual consistency as a first-class design decision, not a compromise. If you don't redesign how data changes propagate, you get silent inconsistency in production and no equivalent of a rollback.

**2. The distributed monolith.** The most common failure mode — you split the code into services but keep them tightly coupled: a shared database, chatty synchronous call chains, every request fanning out across five services before returning. You now have all the costs of distribution — latency, partial failure, debugging across service boundaries — and none of the benefits: you can't deploy or scale services independently. This happens when boundaries are drawn along technical layers (controllers, services, repositories) instead of business capabilities with explicit, stable contracts.

**3. Network reality.** In a monolith a function call either returns or throws, synchronously, in microseconds. Across a network, calls can hang, duplicate, or arrive out of order. That means timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, idempotent handlers, and backpressure become mandatory code — not afterthoughts. New failure modes appear that simply didn't exist before: partial failure, cascading timeouts, retry storms. Kleppmann's *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* covers exactly this shift, and it's worth internalizing before writing the first service.

**4. Operational complexity.** One deployable becomes N services with N deployment pipelines, N sets of logs, N databases, N ways to misconfigure secrets. If your monitoring is not already strong — distributed tracing, correlated logs, metrics per service — you will be debugging blind across process and machine boundaries. Teams routinely underinvest here and then spend months unable to answer "which service caused that failure?"

**5. Testing and versioning.** End-to-end tests across services become slow and flaky; contract-based testing (consumer-driven contracts) becomes necessary. Versioning — how a service changes its API without breaking consumers, how it coordinates a rollout across services — needs a real strategy, not an assumption.

**6. Organizational cost.** Every service boundary is also a team boundary. If teams can't own their services end-to-end — build, deploy, operate — coordination overhead explodes and nothing ships. Doing a microservices migration without restructuring ownership into independent, accountable teams is manufacturing a distributed monolith with extra meetings.

The strategic framing worth holding: the migration should be driven by a concrete constraint — deployment velocity, scaling isolation for one hot path, or one team's autonomy — not by architecture fashion. The disciplined route is the strangler fig pattern: carve off one service at a time, starting with the cleanest business boundary, keeping a stable interface, and letting the monolith shrink gradually. And it's worth asking honestly before starting whether the monolith is actually the constraint — many migrations are two years of effort ending in a system with more moving parts and the same coupling.
