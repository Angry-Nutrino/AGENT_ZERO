### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control, and when each is the right choice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Optimistic concurrency assumes conflicts are rare. You read a record, modify it locally, then on write you check whether the version or timestamp changed since your read — if it did, you abort and retry. Optimistic works when contention is low, operations are short, and retries are cheap. It maximises throughput because no locks are held during the read-modify-write gap. Good fit: read-heavy workloads, web apps where users edit distinct records, collaboration tools with low collision probability.

Pessimistic concurrency assumes conflicts are likely. You lock the record at read time and hold the lock until the transaction completes — reads block, writes block, no one else touches it. Pessimistic works when contention is high, the cost of a retry is unacceptable (payment processing, inventory deduction), or operations span user interaction where you can't let the record drift. Highest correctness guarantee, lowest throughput under contention.

Your choice reduces to: **cost of a conflict versus cost of a lock.** If contention is under ~20% and retry is cheap, go optimistic — you leave throughput on the table otherwise. If even one conflict causes a visible user-facing error or a cascading failure, go pessimistic and design the lock granularity carefully. Your vault dedup scenario from last time was textbook pessimistic territory — a silent corruption is worse than a brief lock.

### Q10 [CHAT] — What is the difference between a process and a thread, and when would you use multiprocessing vs threading in Python? Answer from your training knowledge.

Processes have isolated virtual address spaces, separate file descriptor tables, and independent PID/PGID. Threads share the address space, heap, code/data segments, and open file descriptors — they diverge only on stack, registers, program counter, and per-thread errno. Context switching between threads is cheaper because the MMU does not need to flush the TLB.

In Python, the GIL complicates the heuristic. Multiprocessing bypasses the GIL entirely — use it for CPU-bound work (numerical computation, image processing, video encoding) or when fault isolation matters. Threading is cooperative GIL-releasing — use it for I/O-bound workloads (HTTP requests, file reads, database queries) where the GIL is yielded during blocking syscalls, or for shared-state convenience on data you don't need to serialize between processes. If you need true parallelism *and* shared state on the same machine, the answer is usually a process pool with explicit IPC — the GIL makes threads a false promise for compute.

### Q15 [CHAT] — Explain tail latency (p99/p999): why it dominates user experience in distributed services more than average latency, and one architectural technique to tame it. Answer from your training knowledge.

Tail latency is the long tail of the latency distribution — the slowest 1% (p99) or 0.1% (p999) of requests. Average latency tells you what a typical request experiences; tail latency tells you what the unluckiest user feels. In a distributed service, a single user request fans out to dozens or hundreds of microservices or shards. The user's total latency is determined by the *slowest* of those parallel dependencies, not the average — so even if 99% of sub-requests complete in 10ms, a user whose fan-out hits that 1% slow shard on even one backend sees p99 total. Multiply that by the request volume: at 10,000 requests/second, p99 slowness affects 100 users per second, every second. Averages hide this — they're dominated by the fast majority, so ops teams miss that 1% of users are having a consistently bad experience until those users churn.

The canonical architectural hedge is **hedged requests** (within-request redundancy). Send the same read request to two replicas, accept whichever responds first, cancel the other. This squares the tail: if a single replica has a 1% chance of being slow, the chance *both* replicas are slow drops to 0.01%. Google's Jeff Dean found that just a 10ms delay in hedged dispatch (wait 10ms after the primary, then issue the hedge) covered most tail events without doubling load — most hedges never fire. The cost isn't free (2x read load in the worst case), but for user-facing reads, the latency guarantee is worth the overhead. Capped with a per-request budget and tied to a circuit breaker on the hedge path, you contain the blast radius.
