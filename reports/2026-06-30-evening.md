### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees has three distinct sources, and the costs hit different resources.

**Sources:**

1. **Compaction cascades** — Every level merge (L0→L1, L1→L2, etc.) reads, merges, and writes back a sorted run. An SSTable at level i is rewritten 1–3 times total as it moves to the bottom level, but size-amplification factors and overlapping ranges mean the total write traffic is typically 5–20× the original data inserted. Leveled compaction (RocksDB default) trades lower read amplification for higher write overhead; size-tiered (Cassandra default) reverses the trade.

2. **Bloom filter and index rebuilds** — Each newly written SSTable gets its own Bloom filter and sparse index. These are computed during compaction, not read from the input files. For workloads with many small SSTables, this metadata write can be 5–10% of compaction I/O.

3. **Write-ahead log** — The WAL is sequential and fast, but it's still writing every mutation twice (once to WAL, once to the memtable, then again during flush to L0). With sync=1 for durability, the WAL dominates tail latency under contention.

**Practical costs:**

- **SSD endurance**: Consumer NVMe drives (500–1500 TBW) can die in 1–2 years under write-heavy LSM workloads. RocksDB on a 1TB Samsung 980 Pro (600 TBW) writing 100 GB/day will exhaust rated life in ~16 years — fine. But at 500 GB/day (sustained writes/stress), that drops to ~3 years. Enterprise Optane or high-endurance (5–30 PBW) drives are the real answer here.

- **CPU and memory pressure**: Compaction threads consume CPU. At 100K writes/second with 10× amplification, your kernel is moving 1 GB/s through the I/O stack just for compaction, and your compaction worker threads are burning cores that could serve reads.

- **P99 latency spikes**: Compaction steals disk bandwidth. A compaction burst on a shared NVMe can double read tail latency for 100–500ms. RocksDB's `rate_limiter` mitigates this by throttling compaction I/O to a target bandwidth, but that then extends compaction wall-clock time and risks backpressure on writes if memtables fill faster than compaction drains.

- **Space amplification**: Leveled compaction keeps space overhead at 10–20% of logical data size, size-tiered can reach 50–100%. For a 1TB dataset on a 1.2TB drive, that difference is the margin between "works" and "out of disk, compaction stalls, reads degrade to O(n) scans."

The canonical number: LSM write amplification is usually 5–20×, with leveled compaction at the high end and tiered compaction at the low end. The cost is you trade write budget for read efficiency and space efficiency — and on modern NVMe, the endurance concern is real for write-heavy workloads that run 24/7.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

Two-phase locking: writers block readers. MVCC: every write creates a new version, readers see a snapshot at transaction start, so the reader never waits for an in-progress write.

The garbage: dead row versions — old tuples or pages that are no longer visible to any active transaction. PostgreSQL calls them dead tuples; InnoDB buries them in the undo log until the purge thread reclaims the space. The more writers, the faster they accumulate. If the vacuum or purge falls behind, table bloat drives up I/O, index scans degrade, and the system starts thrashing just to skip the dead versions it hasn't cleaned yet.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability — not a rewrite, but a gradual extraction of bounded contexts from a single deployment unit to distributed ones.

The four main risks:

1. **Network becomes the new database.** What were in-process method calls become fragile, latency-exposed, and failure-prone RPCs. Distributed transactions fail in practice. You end up needing sagas, retry budgets, circuit breakers, and idempotency keys — concerns the monolith never had.

2. **Data consistency breaks.** The monolith's ACID transactions across multiple tables become eventual consistency across service boundaries. Teams that haven't internalized the fallacies of distributed computing start leaking data, double-charging users, or serving stale reads. The trade-off is real — and most teams discover the pain *after* the split.

3. **Operational surface area multiplies.** One monolith becomes 10-30 services, each with its own build, deploy, monitoring, logging, and alerting. Without mature CI/CD, container orchestration, structured logging, and distributed tracing *already in place*, the team spends more time keeping the system running than delivering features.

4. **Organizational Conway's Law backlash.** If the team isn't already structured around bounded contexts, the technical split forces organizational change — or the org chart corrupts the service boundaries. The most common failure pattern is a "distributed monolith": separate services deployed independently but tightly coupled through shared databases, shared schemas, or synchronous call chains. You get all the cost of distribution with none of the benefit.
