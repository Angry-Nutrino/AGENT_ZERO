### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from three cascading sources. First, every write hits the in-memory memtable, and when that memtable flushes to an SSTable on disk, the entire data structure is written — not just the new key. Second, leveled compaction reads N SSTables from level *i*, merges them with one SSTable from level *i+1*, and writes out new SSTables to level *i+1*, so the same logical key is written and rewritten across successive levels as it sinks downward. Third, because LSM-trees are append-only, deletes and updates don't overwrite in place; they produce tombstones and new versions that also get compacted and rewritten later.

In practice, the write amplification factor typically ranges from 10x–20x for leveled compaction and 30x–60x for size-tiered compaction. That means for each 1 MB your application writes, 10–20 MB actually touches disk. The costs are real: reduced SSD lifespan (especially on TLC/QLC drives with limited program/erase cycles), contention for disk bandwidth that degrades read latency during compaction, and write stalls when compaction falls behind and the memtable fills up before it can flush. RocksDB mitigates this with tiered compaction strategies and `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes`, but the fundamental tradeoff — write amplification in exchange for excellent read performance and bounded space amplification — is inherent to the design. You choose LSM when writes dominate and you can budget the disk wear.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each transaction a consistent snapshot at its start time. A reader sees the version of each row that was committed as of that snapshot — never the uncommitted writes of an in-flight writer. The writer writes a new version (tagged with its transaction ID) alongside the old one. The reader reads the old version. Neither waits on the other's lock.

The garbage is **dead row versions** — versions that are no longer visible to any active or future transaction. Every update or delete produces one. They accumulate until a **vacuum** process (PostgreSQL `VACUUM`, MySQL purge thread) marks their space reusable. The vacuum must also clean the index entries that pointed to those dead versions, or index scans will bloat and slow.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability.

The main risks:

**Distributed complexity.** Network latency, partial failure, and eventual consistency replace in-memory calls. A monolith's ACID guarantee becomes a distributed saga problem. Teams that haven't internalized the fallacies of distributed computing will ship brittle systems.

**Domain boundary errors.** The hardest cut is finding the right service boundaries. Split too coarse and you've just got a networked monolith with extra latency. Split too fine and you're managing orchestration for what should be a function call. Both waste the investment.

**Data coupling.** The monolith's shared database was a crutch — once services own their data, every cross-service query becomes a coordination problem. Teams often try to keep joins alive via sync calls, which collapses independence. The discipline of event-driven state propagation is a prerequisite, not an optimization.

**Transactional failure modes.** No distributed transaction framework saves you from the hard part: deciding what to do when service A committed and service B failed. The gap between "two-phase commit" and "saga pattern with compensating transactions" is where real data loss happens.

**Observability debt.** A monolith has one log stream, one latency profile, one crash. A microservices system has N of each, plus the network between them. If you don't have distributed tracing, structured logging, and health-check wiring *before* the split, you're debugging blind.

**Performance regression.** The monolith's in-process call took microseconds. The equivalent service call adds serialization, network I/O, and deserialization — typically 1-10ms added per hop. A single user action that traverses six services just lost you 6-60ms minimum. Teams that don't account for this often end up with a system that's slower and more expensive than the monolith they replaced.

The common thread across all of these: distributed systems amplify every flaw in your testing, observability, and failure-handling practices. A monolith hides those flaws behind in-process guarantees. Microservices exposes them.
