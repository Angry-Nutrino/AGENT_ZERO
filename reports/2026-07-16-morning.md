### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control, and when each is the right choice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Optimistic concurrency assumes conflicts are rare — you read, work, write, and only at commit do you check if the underlying data changed (via version numbers or timestamps). If it did, you abort and retry. Pessimistic assumes conflicts are likely — you lock the resource on read, hold it through the transaction, and release only when done.

**Optimistic wins when:** low contention, short transactions, read-heavy workloads, or when you can't afford long-held locks. Version vectors and vector clocks are the cousin patterns that handle the distributed case without a single coordinator. Good fit for collaborative editing (OT/CRDTs), document stores, and user-facing features where retry is cheap and invisible.

**Pessimistic wins when:** contention is high, writes are frequent, or the cost of a conflict is catastrophic — banking transfers, inventory reservation, seat allocation. If retry means lost revenue or user trust, you take the lock overhead.

Between them: latency profile. Optimistic degrades under contention (retries spike) but has zero locking overhead in the happy path. Pessimistic has constant overhead per transaction regardless of contention but caps worst-case latency. The tension between them is the same as between conflict-free replicated data types and consensus-based replication — whether you accept the possibility of divergence and reconcile later, or pay the synchronization cost upfront.

The real answer is never "always one." It's knowing your access patterns well enough to measure the cost of a retry against the cost of a lock.

### Q10 [CHAT] — What is the difference between a process and a thread, and when would you use multiprocessing vs threading in Python? Answer from your training knowledge.

Processes have isolated address spaces, file descriptors, and signal handlers — fork is heavy. Threads share all of that within a single process, with their own stack, registers, and program counter. Context-switching a thread is on the order of microseconds; a process fork is milliseconds plus the copy-on-write page table cost.

Multiprocessing in Python is for CPU-bound work — the GIL lets exactly one thread hold the CPython bytecode lock at a time, so threading can't parallelise compute. Multiprocessing spawns separate interpreters, each with its own GIL, giving you true parallelism per core. For example, iterating 10 million elements across 8 CPU cores: `Pool.map` will saturate them; 8 threads will thrash the GIL and run roughly the same wall time as one thread.

Threading is for I/O-bound work where you spend most of your time waiting on the kernel — network requests, disk reads, database queries. The GIL is released during I/O waits (the `select`/`epoll` syscall path), so threads can overlap latency. `concurrent.futures.ThreadPoolExecutor` with 20 workers handling HTTP responses will finish far faster than iterating them sequentially.

There's a third pattern worth noting: hybrid, for workloads that are both compute- and I/O-heavy. You use `ProcessPoolExecutor` for the compute chunks, and inside each process, a `ThreadPoolExecutor` for the I/O. The GIL problem doesn't exist within a subprocess because each subprocess has its own GIL, and the I/O is still overlapped. But the serialisation cost of pickling data across the process boundary is real — that's the trade-off you accept for the parallelism.

### Q15 [CHAT] — Explain tail latency (p99/p999): why it dominates user experience in distributed services more than average latency, and one architectural technique to tame it. Answer from your training knowledge.

Average latency hides the worst-case experience — tail latency (p99/p999) captures the slowest 1% or 0.1% of requests, which every user hits eventually when load scales. In a distributed service, a single user request fans out across dozens or hundreds of microservices; the *slowest* service in the chain determines the user's total latency. Even if each service averages 10ms, the 99th percentile at every hop means your p99 end-to-end is a multiplication of extremes, not an addition of averages. The user with the bad day is you, every N requests.

One architectural technique: **hedged requests** (also called raced requests). Send the same request to two replicas in parallel, accept whichever answers first, cancel the laggard. This converts a p99 latency problem into a p50-with-retry — the probability of both replicas being slow simultaneously is p99² (0.01%). The cost is 2× resource consumption at the tail, which is far cheaper than the revenue loss from timeout UX. Same principle as redundant disk writes in RAID — you pay in hardware, you win in consistency of experience.
