### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Three sources: writes to the memtable then to SSTable on flush, compaction merging overlapping SSTables, and repeated compaction of the same data as it moves through levels (Level-0 → Level-1 → ...).

Costs in practice — write throughput drops 10–40× compared to sequential disk bandwidth. On consumer SSDs, sustained compaction turns a 500 MB/s drive into 50–100 MB/s effective write speed. The Samsung 990 Pro, with ~4,000 TBW endurance, loses 3–5 years of rated life under heavy LSM workloads (RocksDB with aggressive compaction). Latency spikes during major compactions (level-6 merges) can reach 200–800 ms on HDD, 20–60 ms on NVMe, because the compaction thread saturates the I/O queue.

The trade-off is intentional: you accept write amplification for read amplification near zero and bounded space amplification (~1.1–1.5×). Tuning the compaction policy — size-tiered vs. level, number of levels, SSTable size — is where you trade write amplification against read performance and space overhead.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each transaction a point-in-time snapshot of the database at the moment it begins. A reader reads the version of each row that was committed *before* its snapshot — never the uncommitted write of an in-flight writer. A writer creates a new version of the row (or marks the old one as obsolete in the index) without touching the existing visible version. The reader sees the old version; the writer proceeds on its own. No locks, no waiting, no contention.

What it leaves behind is **garbage versions** — rows that are no longer visible to *any* active or future transaction because they've been superseded by a later update or deletion. This accumulates as:

- Dead row versions in heap pages (PostgreSQL's bloat, InnoDB's undo log records).
- Obsolete index entries that still point at those dead rows (index bloat, which degrades B-tree scan efficiency).
- Version chains or undo segments that must be traversed but will never be followed to a visible result.

The cleanup mechanism is **VACUUM** in PostgreSQL (marks space reusable, can return it to the OS if aggressive enough) and the **purge/undo truncation** thread in MySQL/InnoDB (recycles undo log space and coalesces pages). In both cases, a concurrent scanner — the *snapshot of the oldest active transaction* — defines the horizon. Any version older than that horizon is safe to reclaim. If a long-running read transaction holds its snapshot open indefinitely, garbage piles up until it forces autovacuum or manual intervention.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and bounded context.

The main risks:

- **Network as the new primitive**: In a monolith, a function call is reliable, low-latency, and synchronous. In microservices, every call becomes a network hop — with latency, partial failure, and no guarantee of delivery. Most teams don't have the observability or circuit-breaking infrastructure in place before they start splitting.

- **Distributed data ownership**: The monolith's single database transactions become distributed sagas or eventual consistency. Teams often underestimate how much application logic depended on ACID guarantees they now have to rebuild.

- **Service boundaries that don't map to reality**: The hardest part is deciding where the cuts go. Cut too coarse and you still have deployment coupling. Cut too fine and you get orchestration spaghetti. The real bounded context often reveals itself only after the third rearchitecture.

- **Operational complexity surge**: One deployable becomes ten. Now you need service discovery, container orchestration, centralized logging, distributed tracing, health checks, rate limiting, and secrets management — all of which were handled by the monolith's runtime environment.

- **People and organizational coupling**: Conway's law is not optional. If you have five services but three teams, the team boundaries will override the service boundaries. The failure mode is teams stepping on each other's deployment cadences because nobody owns the integration contract.

- **The strangler fig pattern done wrong**: Teams extract one service, deploy it, then never come back for the rest — leaving a hybrid that has all the complexity of both architectures and the benefits of neither.
