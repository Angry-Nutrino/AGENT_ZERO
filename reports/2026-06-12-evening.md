### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental design tension: you're trading random writes for sequential ones, but each piece of data gets written multiple times before it settles.

**Where it comes from:**

Three distinct sources stack:

1. **Compaction.** An LSM-tree has multiple levels (L0, L1, L2…). When a level fills up, the system merges its SSTables into the next level. Every key-value pair that lives through multiple compactions gets rewritten each time. The total write amplification from compaction alone is roughly the product of the size ratios between levels — a typical LevelDB-style tiered compaction with 10× size ratios and 4 levels means each byte is rewritten ~10–40 times before it reaches the bottom level. Size-tiered compaction (Cassandra's default) is worse: entire SSTables are rewritten in one shot, and older data gets repeatedly merged into larger and larger tables.

2. **The write-ahead log.** Every write hits the WAL *before* it touches the memtable. That's one extra write per operation on the primary commit path. The WAL is sequential, so the per-byte cost is low, but the volume is identical to the write workload.

3. **Memtable flush.** When the in-memory memtable is full, it's flushed to disk as an L0 SSTable. That's another full write of everything that was in memory. With modestly sized memtables (64–256 MB), this happens frequently under write-heavy loads.

Practical amplification factors: LevelDB/RocksDB with leveled compaction typically sees 10–20× write amplification. Cassandra with size-tiered compaction can hit 30–50× under sustained writes. A single logical write of 1 KB can cause 10–50 KB of actual disk I/O.

**What it costs in practice:**

The dominant cost isn't disk space — it's **I/O bandwidth consumed by background work when your foreground workload is also writing.** During peak write load, compactions compete with the primary write path for the same disk. On HDDs, this means throughput collapses; on SSDs, it means write endurance burns faster and you hit the drive's sustained write limit, causing latency spikes.

Two concrete scenarios:

- **RocksDB under sustained writes.** If you're writing 100 MB/s of user data and hitting 20× write amplification, the system is pushing 2 GB/s through the I/O subsystem. On a single NVMe drive (3–5 GB/s sequential write), that's 40–60% of its theoretical bandwidth consumed by compaction alone. The foreground write latency goes from microseconds to milliseconds as the drive firmware queues internally.

- **Cassandra on SSDs.** Every SSTable compaction triggers full-data rewrites. On a node doing 5,000 writes/sec with 1 KB payloads and 30× amplification, that's 150 MB/s of total write traffic. Under normal load, this works. Under a burst to 20,000 writes/sec, compaction falls behind, stalls accumulate, and the node either blocks new writes or enters hints-handoff territory.

The real cost is rarely disk space. It's the **amplification of peak write load into sustained background I/O that starves reads and drives tail latency.** This is why you tune compaction triggers aggressively — you're not saving space, you're trying to bound the background I/O so it doesn't eat the foreground.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

Two mechanisms, one goal.

MVCC gives each transaction a snapshot of the database at a point in time — usually the transaction's start timestamp or a monotonically increasing transaction ID. When a reader begins, it sees that snapshot. When a writer updates a row, it doesn't overwrite the old data in place. It creates a new version of that row, tagged with the writer's transaction ID. The old version remains visible to any reader whose snapshot predates the writer's commit.

Result: a reader never waits for a writer, and a writer never waits for a reader. The writer appends; the reader consults a visibility check — "is this version's creation timestamp ≤ my snapshot, and has this version not been deleted by a transaction committed after my snapshot?" If yes, the reader sees it. If no, it walks the chain of older versions until it finds one that satisfies the snapshot.

The garbage: abandoned row versions. Every update or delete leaves behind an older version that no active transaction will ever need again. Over time these accumulate into what's called *version churn* — a growing chain of dead tuples in every updated page. In PostgreSQL this is bloat; in InnoDB it's the undo log and the history list. Both grow unbounded until cleaned.

Cleanup is a separate process: a vacuum (PostgreSQL), a purge thread (MySQL/InnoDB), or a compaction cycle (Oracle, SQL Server with snapshot isolation). It scans for versions whose transaction ID is older than the oldest still-running transaction's snapshot, then reclaims that space. Until that happens, index scans pay the cost of skipping dead versions, and the table's on-disk footprint expands.

The cost of never waiting is never-free reads — and the debt comes due in garbage.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability.

The main risks, ordered by how often they actually kill migrations:

**1. Network becomes the new database.** Your monolith made hundreds of local function calls per request. Split those into network calls and suddenly you have latency cascades, partial failures, retry storms, and timeouts where you never had them before. Most teams underestimate this by 3-5x.

**2. Distributed data consistency.** You lose ACID transactions. What was a single `COMMIT` now spans multiple databases with no rollback guarantee. Teams reach for two-phase commit (bad idea) or sagas (correct but operationally complex) and often get neither right on the first try.

**3. Observability debt.** In a monolith, a single stack trace told you the whole story. In microservices, a user-reported error requires correlating traces across 8 services, each with its own logging format, timestamp drift, and sampling rate. Most migrations don't invest in distributed tracing until after a production incident forces it.

**4. Premature service boundaries.** The first service extracted is almost never the right one. Teams pick the easiest cut line, not the right domain boundary, and end up with chatty services that need synchronized deploys — defeating the independence goal.

**5. Operational blast radius.** Kubernetes, service meshes, API gateways, secret management, container registries — the operational surface area expands faster than the development surface area. A team that managed one deploy pipeline now manages ten, and each one can fail in unique ways.

**6. Data migration.** You can't just `SELECT * INTO new_db`. Synchronizing millions of rows while the monolith is still writing, handling backfill conflicts, and deciding ownership of shared reference data — this is where migrations stall for months.

The pattern that consistently works: extract one service at a time starting from the outer edges (stateless, query-only, pure computation) toward the core. The Strangler Fig pattern. Never big-bang. And instrument everything before you migrate a single line — if you can't tell whether the new service is slower than the monolith, you're not ready to cut over.
