### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fact that the same data is written multiple times over its lifetime. Each write first hits the write-ahead log, then the in-memory memtable, and when that memtable flushes to disk as an SSTable it's written again. Then compaction — the hierarchical merging of SSTables to maintain sorted order and reclaim space — reads and re-writes older data that hasn't changed at all. A single key insertion can generate 10–50x the write traffic to the physical disk vs. the logical write the application issued.

In practice, the costs are threefold:

1. **Wear on flash storage** — SSDs have finite program/erase cycles. Every amplified byte burns P/E budget. On write-heavy workloads (time series, metrics ingestion, event logs), an LSM engine can shorten SSD lifespan from years to months if compaction isn't tuned. This is why LevelDB/RocksDB offer `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes` — to avoid the worst multiplier at large levels.

2. **CPU as the hidden bottleneck** — Compaction threads consume cores doing merge-sort, decompression, recompression, and checksum verification. If write throughput saturates the compaction threads faster than they can keep up, the write stalls cascade: the memtable fills, writes block, P99 latency spikes. This is the cost most operators feel first — not disk wear but unpredictable latency under sustained writes.

3. **IO bandwidth cannibalization** — While compaction is rewriting Level-2 SSTables, the read path needs to binary-search those same files for cache misses. On spinning disks (still common in cold tier / archival), the seek contention between compaction and reads destroys throughput. Even on NVMe, if compaction and foreground writes share a queue depth budget, read latency degrades.

The actual multiplier depends on the compaction strategy. Size-tiered (Cassandra default) can hit 50–100x on overwrite-heavy workloads. Leveled compaction (RocksDB default, leveled-to-tiered) aims for 5–15x. Universal compaction trades some amplification for better space amplification. But zero write amplification only exists outside of LSM-world — that's the cost of keeping writes append-only and reads sorted.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC works by giving each reader a consistent snapshot of the database at a point in time. When a writer starts modifying rows, it creates new versions of those rows — it doesn't touch the existing ones that readers are still looking at. The reader's snapshot continues to point at the old version. Neither waits on the other.

The garbage is **dead row versions** — old versions that are no longer visible to any active transaction but still occupy space in the storage engine. This accumulates as bloat in the index and heap structures. PostgreSQL calls its cleanup process **VACUUM** — it marks the dead tuples as reusable and, depending on the implementation, may physically compact them. Without it, the table grows monotonically and performance degrades from increased I/O on every scan.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and domain logic.

The main risks when splitting a monolith:

1. **Network as the new primitive** — in-method calls become remote calls. Latency, partial failure, and timeouts are now first-class problems that didn't exist before. The system must handle retries, circuit breakers, and fallbacks where a simple try-except used to suffice.

2. **Data consistency breaks** — the monolith's single ACID transaction becomes a distributed transaction problem. You now deal with eventual consistency, sagas, and compensation logic. Most teams underestimate how much their business logic tacitly depended on database-level consistency guarantees.

3. **Boundary mistakes are expensive** — the wrong service boundary (splitting on technical layers instead of business domains, or carving too fine) creates chatty cross-service calls that are slower and harder to reason about than the monolith was. Re-merging services is painful and rare in practice.

4. **Operational complexity multiplies** — one deployable becomes N. You now need service discovery, coordinated deployments, centralized logging, distributed tracing, per-service monitoring, and infrastructure automation just to keep the lights on. If the team doesn't have that baseline operational maturity, the migration adds debt faster than it pays down.

5. **Organizational coupling** — Conway's law is not optional. If the team structure doesn't match the service boundaries, you get coordination overhead that kills the independence argument. Two teams maintaining the same service is a monolith with extra network calls.

6. **The strangler fig pattern is not free** — the common recommended approach (incrementally routing traffic to new services while keeping the monolith alive) requires disciplined feature flags, backward-compatible interfaces, and the discipline to retire dead code from the monolith. Most teams stop at "migrated" and leave the monolith as a rotting parallel system that nobody touches.

The migration pays off when the monolith's deployment bottleneck is actively costing the business. If the risk of splitting is higher than the cost of staying monolithic, the right answer is to wait.
