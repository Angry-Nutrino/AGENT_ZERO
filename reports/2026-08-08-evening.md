### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from one unavoidable fact: the data isn't written in place, it's rewritten — over and over, at multiple levels — until it's eventually read.

**Where it comes from**

Three distinct sources:

1. **Compaction.** The core cost. When a run merges into the next level, every key-value pair that participates is read and written again. With level-based compaction and a size ratio of ~10× per level, each byte is written roughly once per level it passes through. That's the ~10–30× write amplification you see in real deployments.

2. **Memtable flush.** The in-memory table becomes an immutable SSTable. That write isn't optional, but it's the cheapest leg — the data was arriving anyway.

3. **WAL.** Every write is appended once to the log before it touches the memtable. That's the +1 on top of every amplification figure. Without it you lose durability; with it, you pay a write for every write.

**What it costs in practice**

- **SSD endurance.** Consumer TLC flash is rated for roughly 600–1,000 program/erase cycles, 1,500+ for enterprise. At 20× write amplification, a 256 GB drive's 150 TBW endurance becomes ~7.5 TB of actual user data before the drive is suspect. Heavy write workloads can burn through years of endurance in months, which is why RocksDB defaults assume you care about this number.
- **Write latency tail.** The memtable absorbs bursty writes well, but L0→L1 compaction runs synchronously with incoming traffic. When compactions fall behind, you get write stalls — the backend throttle that turns a 500 µs write into a 50 ms pause. This is almost always the first pain point in practice, often before endurance is even on the radar.
- **CPU and I/O that isn't "real."** A 2 MB/s application write rate can easily translate to 40–60 MB/s of actual disk traffic. That's bandwidth, queue depth, and CPU spent on data movement the application never asked for, competing with reads and background maintenance for the same device.

**The trade you're actually buying**

Every LSM engine trades this write amplification for two things: write *latency* (in-place B-tree updates touch scattered pages; LSM appends are sequential) and read amplification at the other end (bloom filters, block caches, and level structure exist to claw that back). All three costs — write amp, read amp, space amp — are one knob in a three-way balance. Tune the compaction strategy, the size ratio, and the level count, and you're only ever moving the pain, not removing it.

RocksDB's default `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes` and a size ratio around 10 yield ~10× write amplification. Push to 20× with a small memtable and aggressive leveling; trim toward 4–5× with universal compaction and bigger levels — at the price of read latency and space amplification. There is no free configuration.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC lets readers and writers coexist without blocking because the database doesn't let a writer mutate a row in place — it creates a new version. The reader transaction isn't handed a live snapshot of shared state, it's handed a version of the database frozen at its start time, so a concurrent writer can add a new version without ever touching what the reader is reading. The reader checks each row version's visibility against its own snapshot conditions — created-before-my-start, not deleted-before-my-start — and skips anything that doesn't fit. No locks needed for reads, no waiting.

The garbage is the old row versions that are no longer visible to any active transaction. Every update and delete creates a dead version; as long as even one long-running transaction still holds an old snapshot, those versions have to stay in the table. The cleanup job is a vacuum-like process (Postgres calls it VACUUM, in-place systems that use undo logs call it purge/rollback): it finds versions that are invisible to every transaction, reclaims the space for reuse, and keeps the version chain from growing without bound. If it doesn't keep up — too many updates, or one long transaction pinning old versions — bloat grows, index scans slow down, and the table balloons.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

**What it is.** A microservices migration is the architectural transformation of a single, one-shot deployable monolith into a set of small, independently deployable services, each owning its own data, communicating over the network via explicit contracts (HTTP/gRPC/events). The goal is independent scaling, independent deployment, and team autonomy. The hard part is that you're trading an in-process function call — cheap, reliable, atomic — for a network round trip that can fail, be slow, or arrive twice.

**The main risks, roughly in order of how much damage they do:**

1. **Losing ACID across boundaries.** The monolith gives you one transaction spanning many tables. Split that and you get distributed transactions, which classic 2PC doesn't scale and modern systems avoid. You're forced into sagas, the outbox pattern, and eventual consistency. This is not an implementation detail — it changes what "correct" means for your entire data layer. Teams routinely underestimate this and it's the number one cause of failed migrations.

2. **The shared database cheat.** To avoid the pain above, teams keep one DB and "split" the code around it. Result: services that are still coupled at the schema level and can't deploy independently anyway. That's not microservices — it's a distributed monolith with extra latency and worse failure modes.

3. **Network fallacies in practice.** The network is not reliable, not zero-latency, not secure, and topology changes. You need timeouts, retries, idempotency, circuit breakers, and bulkheads — and you need them designed in, not bolted on after the first outage.

4. **Data consistency and reconciliation.** Two services now each hold a copy of related data. Who owns the source of truth? What happens to the local cache when the source updates? Every "simple" cross-service read becomes a consistency decision.

5. **Contract drift and versioning.** Services evolve on independent release cycles. Any breaking API change ripples into every consumer. You need explicit versioning strategies and ideally consumer-driven contract tests — or you get the classic "works on my service, breaks in production" chain.

6. **Operational and observability burden.** One service = one log stream, one health check. Twenty services = distributed tracing, correlation IDs, log aggregation, metrics per service, alerting on SLOs not just "is it up." Teams that skip investing in observability up front are flying blind within a month. Also: more deployments, more pipelines, more secrets to rotate, more security surface to defend.

7. **Organizational coupling (Conway's Law).** If your team structure doesn't mirror the service boundaries, you'll generate coordination overhead that eats the autonomy benefit. Service ownership requires a team that can actually own it end to end.

8. **The big-bang temptation.** Trying to split everything at once maximizes risk and makes rollback nearly impossible. The sane path is the strangler fig pattern — carve off one bounded context at a time behind the existing app, verify, repeat. The opposite risk is starting and never finishing, leaving a zombie monolith half-wrapped in services with all the cost and none of the payoff.

9. **Performance regression disguised as architecture.** Serialization, round trips, and chatty service-to-service calls add real latency. A well-targeted monolith will often out-perform a badly-split microservices system on the same hardware.

**The honest bottom line.** Microservices are not an upgrade — they're a trade. You buy independent deployability and scalability at the price of distributed-systems complexity. The migration is only worth it when the monolith is genuinely blocking one of those goals, *or* when the codebase size and team structure have outgrown a single deploy unit. If none of those pressures exist, a modular monolith gets you most of the discipline with a fraction of the risk. When you do migrate, the rules are: find boundaries with domain-driven design (bounded contexts, not table groups), extract incrementally via strangler fig, design for eventual consistency from day one, and build observability before you split the second service.
