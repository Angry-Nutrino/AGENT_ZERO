### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental design trade-off of converting random writes into sequential ones by paying the cost of rewriting data multiple times.

**Where it comes from:**

Three distinct stages generate it:

1. **Memtable flush to L0.** When the in-memory memtable is full, it's written to disk as a sorted SSTable. Every piece of data that arrived as a write must be written once here — this is the baseline, unavoidable write.

2. **Compaction.** As SSTables accumulate in L0, the system must merge them to maintain read performance and reclaim space. Each compaction reads N SSTables from level *i* and writes one merged SSTable to level *i+1*. The data that was already written in step 1 is rewritten in step 2. In Leveled compaction (LevelDB / RocksDB default), a single key-value pair may be promoted through 7 levels — written anew at each level. A write of 1 KB can become 10–20 KB of actual disk I/O by the time it settles in the lowest level. That's a 10–20× write amplification factor.

3. **WAL.** Before any memtable write, the data is written to the write-ahead log for crash recovery. Add one more write per mutation.

**The practical costs:**

**Throughput ceiling on consumer SSDs.** A Samsung 990 Pro can sustain about 5 GB/s sequential writes, but you have to share that bandwidth between the user's writes and compaction's writes. At 10× amplification, every 100 MB/s of user writes generates 900 MB/s of compaction I/O. Cross 500 MB/s of sustained user writes and you're saturating the SSD — latency spikes, the drive starts thermal throttling, and effective write speed collapses.

**Drive lifespan.** Data sheets quote TBW (Total Bytes Written) as a lifetime limit — 1,200 TBW on a 2 TB 990 Pro. Without amplification, you'd need to write 1.2 PB to wear it out. At 10× amplification, an application that writes 100 GB/day of actual data writes 1 TB/day to the flash. That 1,200 TBW rating becomes 3.3 years of life instead of 33.

**The latency tail.** During a large compaction, the compaction thread is saturating the I/O queue. A foreground read that needs to consult the same disk stalls behind compaction reads and writes. P99 latency can jump from 1 ms to 100 ms+ during a compaction storm. This is why Cassandra's Leveled Compaction Strategy has a reputation for "compaction death spirals" under sustained heavy writes — compaction can't keep up, the write path backs up, user-facing latency breaks.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each read transaction a snapshot of the database at a point in time — typically the transaction's start timestamp or the last committed transaction ID before it. When a writer modifies a row, it doesn't overwrite the existing row in place; instead it creates a new version with the writer's transaction ID. The old version remains accessible to any reader whose snapshot predates that writer's commit. So the reader reads its snapshot version without ever taking a lock, and the writer commits its new version without waiting for the reader to finish.

The garbage this creates is **dead row versions** — old tuples that are no longer visible to any active or future transaction. These accumulate in the table's storage pages as outdated copies. PostgreSQL calls them *dead tuples*; they bloat the table, fragment indexes, and degrade query performance until a **VACUUM** pass reclaims the space. Without cleanup, the table grows unboundedly regardless of how many logical rows exist.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability.

The main risks, in order of how often they kill the project:

1. **Network as the new primitive** — A monolith's in-process calls become remote calls with latency, partial failure, and timeouts. Teams who haven't internalized the fallacies of distributed computing end up with chatty services that are slower and less reliable than the monolith they replaced.

2. **Data coupling via shared database** — The most common failure pattern. Teams decompose the application code but leave a single shared database, creating hidden transactional dependencies. A service can't deploy independently if a schema change in another service breaks its queries. You haven't migrated until data ownership is per-service.

3. **Distributed transactions leak consistency** — Two-phase commit across services destroys availability. Sagas and eventual consistency are the replacements, but most teams underestimate how much of their business logic relied on ACID guarantees they were taking for granted. The first saga failure at 3 AM that leaves an order in limbo is when the reality check hits.

4. **Observability debt** — A monolith has one log stream, one process to monitor, one place to look when something breaks. A distributed system has N services × M instances, each producing logs, metrics, and traces in isolation. Teams often build the services first and add observability after the first outage they can't debug.

5. **Organizational Conway's Law mismatch** — If your team structure doesn't match the service boundaries you've drawn, you get coordination overhead that negates the autonomy you were after. A service owned by two teams is just a distributed monolith with more ceremony.

6. **Cutting the monolith in the wrong places** — Splitting by technical layer (frontend, backend, database) instead of business capability creates cross-service changes for every feature. The right cut is bounded contexts from Domain-Driven Design — services should change together only when the business domain itself changes.

7. **Premature migration without strangler fig pattern** — Rewriting the entire monolith at once (the "big bang") is the highest-risk approach. The strangled migration pattern — routing individual features to new services one at a time, verifying each, then decommissioning the old code — is the only proven safe approach.
