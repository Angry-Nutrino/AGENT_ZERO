### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental tension between their design and the reality of flash storage: each piece of logical data is physically written multiple times over its lifetime.

**Where it comes from — three sources:**

1. **Compaction.** Every level merge reads data from L₁ (say two 5 MB SSTables), sorts and deduplicates, then writes back a single 10 MB table to L₂. That 10 MB was already written once to the write-ahead log, once to L₁'s memtable flush, and now again to L₂. Each compaction cascades that cost upward through the tiering.

2. **Write-ahead log.** Every write hits the WAL before it touches the memtable. On crash recovery, the WAL is replayed — a full rewrite of everything that was just committed.

3. **Memtable flush to SSTable.** The in-memory sorted structure is drained to disk as the first on-disk copy. This is the one write that's unavoidable; the others are pure overhead from the LSM architecture.

**What it costs in practice:**

On a typical RocksDB setup with leveled compaction (default: 10x per level, 7 levels), write amplification factors range from 10–30×. That means for every 1 MB your application writes, the storage engine writes 10–30 MB physically. On consumer NVMe SSDs rated for 600 TBW, a database ingesting 10 GB/day at 20× amplification burns through 200 GB/day of endurance — dropping the drive's lifespan from ~164 years to ~8 years. In practice, that usually doesn't matter on client hardware, but in a cloud DB instance with provisioned IOPS, you're paying for all 20× of those writes whether you wanted them or not.

The other cost is read amplification: multiple levels must be consulted per point lookup, and range scans merge across tiers. LSM-trees trade _write_ speed for _read_ complexity — the write amplification is the bill you pay for making writes sequential instead of random.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each reader a consistent snapshot of the database as of the instant the transaction began. The reader sees a "versioned" view — when a writer modifies a row, it creates a new version rather than overwriting the old one. The reader simply traverses the snapshot's version chain, so it never waits on a writer's lock and never blocks the writer from creating that new version.

The garbage is **dead row versions** — old tuples that are no longer visible to any active transaction. These accumulate and must be reclaimed by a **vacuum** (PostgreSQL) or **purge** (MySQL InnoDB) process that walks the table, identifies versions whose visibility horizon has passed, and returns that space to the heap. If that cleanup falls behind, you get table bloat, index bloat, and the performance cliff of needing to scan stale versions you'll never read.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of taking a monolithic application — one codebase that handles everything — and decomposing it into independently deployable services, each owning a bounded context and communicating over a network.

Main risks when splitting a monolith:

1. **Network as new failure domain** — what was a function call within the same process becomes an HTTP or RPC call that can time out, be retried, cascade, or silently drop. Latency shifts from microseconds to milliseconds. You introduce partial failure everywhere, and if you haven't designed for it, the system becomes fragile.

2. **Distributed data ownership** — the monolith's shared database gets partitioned. Now you have separate databases that can drift out of sync. Maintaining consistency across services — especially without distributed transactions — requires patterns like saga orchestration or choreography, which are harder to reason about and debug.

3. **Operational surface area explodes** — one deployment pipeline becomes many. One set of logs becomes aggregators, tracing spans, and correlation IDs you never needed before. Monitoring, alerting, and debugging each require significantly more infrastructure and discipline.

4. **Bounded context mistakes** — the most common error. Teams draw service boundaries along technical layers (split by "controllers vs models") instead of business domains (split by "orders vs inventory"). This creates chatty services that can't be deployed independently because they still implicitly depend on each other's schema or behavior.

5. **Premature extraction** — pulling out a service before you understand the monolith's actual coupling points. If you don't know which code paths actually touch which data and in what order, you break transactions that were never documented. The safe path is to extract only when the boundary is well-understood and the monolith is actively causing pain on that axis.

6. **Network latency and data duplication** — what the monolith got with a JOIN now requires either an API call (adding latency) or duplicating data across services (adding sync complexity). Both are trade-offs that teams often underestimate until they're in production.
