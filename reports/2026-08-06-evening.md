### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in an LSM-tree is the ratio between what the application actually writes and what the storage device physically ends up writing. A 1 MB logical write can easily become 10–30 MB of actual flash I/O. The source is the architecture itself: the tree is never updated in place — it's a cascade of rewrites, and every byte you insert gets rewritten several times as it descends.

**Where it comes from**

1. **The memtable flush.** Every write lands in an in-memory buffer first. When that buffer fills, it's flushed to disk as an SSTable. Your data is written to disk once here — that's the first write.

2. **Compaction.** This is the dominant cost. SSTables accumulate in levels. As each level fills, the engine merges its tables with overlapping tables in the next level, sorts the merged result, and writes entirely new SSTables. The old ones are discarded. In a leveled design, a row that lands in L0 will be rewritten once per level as it's promoted downward. With a size ratio of ~10 per level, a 1 MB write at L0 eventually triggers ~10 MB of merging at L1, ~100 MB at L2, and so on — worst case roughly O(B^L) where B is the size ratio and L is the level depth. That's where the 10–30x numbers come from in practice.

3. The **WAL** is a smaller contributor — a second write of every operation for durability — but it's sequential, cheap, and often on faster media, so usually an afterthought.

**What it costs in practice**

- **Flash endurance.** SSDs have finite program/erase cycles. A drive rated for ~300 TBW loses meaningful lifespan under 20x amplification. If your workload writes 100 GB/day logically, the drive sees 2 TB/day. That turns a 5-year drive into a 1–2 year drive if you hammer it continuously.

- **Throughput headroom.** The storage device's write bandwidth is consumed by compaction work, not by your actual writes. A database that logically writes 50 MB/s may need 1 GB/s of raw device bandwidth to sustain it. If you're bandwidth-bound, amplification directly caps your sustained ingestion rate. This is often the first thing you hit in production, before durability even becomes the problem.

- **Latency spikes.** Compaction runs in the background, but when a large compaction job overlaps a burst of incoming writes, both compete for the same I/O queue. P99 latency degrades. This is the classic LSM "compaction stall" — you feel it as unpredictable tails, not steady-state slowness.

- **CPU and memory overhead.** Every merge involves sorting, comparisons, and checksums; every rewritten block costs kernel and filesystem work. Small on modern hardware relative to the I/O cost, but not zero.

**The trade-off you're actually paying for**

LSMs accept write and space amplification in exchange for dramatically lower read amplification than B-trees — sequential writes instead of random, no in-place page updates, no fsync-per-commit latency. B-trees sit at the opposite end of the spectrum: they write each page roughly once, but updates touch random locations and cause their own amplification problems at the page and filesystem level. Between the two, LSM-trees are the right answer for write-heavy ingestion workloads; B-trees win for read-heavy, point-lookup-bound workloads.

**How it's controlled in practice**

- **Size-tiered compaction** (bigger SSTables merged together, less frequently) trades lower write amplification for higher space and read amplification. This is what Cassandra and older HBase deployments used; good for write-heavy but the levels can balloon.
- **Leveled compaction** (RocksDB/LevelDB default) keeps write amplification higher but bounds space amplification tightly.
- RocksDB's practical knobs: larger memtables, more L0 files before compaction kicks in, `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes`, and **Universal compaction** for extremely write-heavy ingest.
- **Blob files / large-value separation** (RocksDB BlobDB, TiKV) keeps big values out of the compaction stream entirely — a single value near a few KB can get rewritten dozens of times otherwise; separating it cuts most of the amplification for that workload.

The engineering answer to "what does it cost" is: it costs endurance, bandwidth headroom, and tail latency, and you accept it because the alternative — a B-tree — costs you on writes instead. The trick in practice is tuning the compaction strategy to the actual read/write ratio of your workload, not taking the defaults.

If you're applying this to the orchestrator's storage layer, the relevant design decision is whether your event log pushes sequential data (good LSM fit) or in-place mutable records (bad fit). Different ingestion patterns, different amplification profiles.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC decouples readers from writers by giving each transaction a consistent snapshot of the database as of a specific point in time, instead of requiring it to acquire shared locks on the data it reads.

**How it works, in essence:**

Each row carries version metadata — typically a creation transaction ID and a deletion transaction ID (a tombstone). When a transaction computes its snapshot, it captures the set of transactions committed at that moment. Visibility is then a simple rule: a version is visible if its creating transaction committed before the snapshot, and its deleting transaction hasn't committed before the snapshot. A reader walks the version chain for a row until it finds the version that was current at its snapshot.

**Why readers don't block writers:**

A writer never mutates a row in place. It inserts a *new* version. The reader keeps seeing the old version it already knows is valid for its snapshot, so the two operate on different physical versions simultaneously. The classic conflict — reader holding a shared lock that a writer needs an exclusive lock for — simply doesn't exist. And in the common case, writers don't block readers either: a writer only contends with *other writers* on the same row, which is resolved by the database detecting a write-write conflict at commit time (or aborting one).

**The garbage:**

1. **Obsolete row versions** — old versions of rows that no active transaction can see anymore. These are the bulk of the problem.
2. **Dead index entries** — index pointers referencing those dead versions. If ignored, indexes bloat and scans degrade.
3. **Expired transaction metadata** — transaction IDs and commit records that are no longer needed to resolve visibility for any live snapshot.

**The cleanup:**

A background process reclaims versions that are older than the *oldest active transaction's* snapshot — anything newer than that must stay, because that transaction could still legitimately read it. PostgreSQL calls this VACUUM; Oracle uses undo segments reclaimed by a background process; SQL Server uses a version store cleaned by a background sweeper. The practical consequence: a long-running transaction pins garbage behind it, which is why "query held open for hours" shows up as table bloat. The system also periodically truncates the transaction ID space so IDs don't wrap.

The design trade-off worth holding onto: MVCC trades storage and cleanup cost for concurrency. Read-heavy workloads win massively; write-heavy workloads with long transactions pay for it in bloat and vacuum pressure.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

**Microservices migration** is the process of decomposing a single, unified application — one codebase, one deployment unit, one shared database — into a set of smaller, independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and exposing a defined API.

The critical word is *process*. It's not a rewrite and it's not a refactor — it's a gradual architectural transformation, usually following the strangler-fig pattern: carve out a bounded context, extract it behind an interface, route traffic to the new service, then delete the old code from the monolith, repeating until the monolith is gone or reduced to a shell.

What makes it architectural rather than merely technical: service boundaries tend to mirror team boundaries (Conway's Law), so the migration is as much an organizational change as a code change.

---

**The main risks when splitting a monolith:**

**1. Distributed data consistency.** The monolith's single database gives you ACID transactions, foreign keys, and joins for free. Break the schema and all of that disappears. Distributed transactions (2PC) are slow and fragile; you fall back to sagas, outbox patterns, idempotency keys, and eventual consistency — all of which are harder to reason about and harder to get right.

**2. Network failures become first-class.** In-process function calls don't time out, partially fail, or return garbage. Network calls do. You inherit the fallacies of distributed computing: latency, packet loss, out-of-order delivery, clock skew. Every service boundary now requires timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, and bulkheads — or a single slow dependency cascades into system-wide failure.

**3. Operational complexity explodes.** One deployable becomes dozens. You now need service discovery, container orchestration, log aggregation, distributed tracing, and a monitoring strategy that can reconstruct a request's path across multiple services. Debugging a distributed system is categorically harder than debugging a monolith.

**4. Latency and performance regression.** Every in-process call becomes serialization + network + deserialization. Chatty interfaces between services amplify this — if your service boundaries require 20 round-trips to fulfill a single user request, you just made your system slower for no benefit.

**5. Wrong boundaries → a distributed monolith.** The most common failure mode. Teams split by *layer* (frontend, backend, database) instead of by *business capability*, or extract services that still share a database, or build deployment-time coupling where services must be released together. Result: all the cost of distribution with none of the independence. This is the worst outcome because it's invisible until it's too late.

**6. The database is the actual monolith.** Schemas resist decomposition. Splitting a shared schema means migrating live data, handling joins that no longer exist, and managing dual-writes during the transition period. Many migrations succeed at the code level and fail at the data level.

**7. Testing complexity multiplies.** Integration tests across service boundaries require either real running services or contract tests and consumer-driven contracts. You trade a reliable in-process test suite for a much more elaborate testing strategy that is harder to run and easier to get wrong.

**8. The transition period is the worst time.** During a strangler migration you run a hybrid: some calls in-process, some over the network, dual-writes, feature flags, temporary bridge code. This is peak complexity — you pay the cost of both architectures simultaneously, and the benefits only materialize at the end. It's a long, expensive journey with a long plateau of no visible payoff.

**9. Premature or unjustified extraction.** If the monolith isn't well-modularized internally, extracting services just moves the mess across a network boundary. If you can't define a clean API against in-process code, you won't magically define one between processes. And if the team is small, the monolith isn't actually a bottleneck, or the domain lacks clear bounded contexts, the migration is pure cost with no return.

---

The honest headline: migration is justified when independent deployability, team scaling, or independent scaling actually require it — not because microservices are fashionable. The evidence-based counterpoint is the modular monolith: a single deployable with strict internal module boundaries. It captures most of the architectural discipline at a fraction of the operational cost. The migration itself should always start there — get the monolith cleanly modularized first, and only extract services where the boundary is genuinely stable and the benefit is real.
