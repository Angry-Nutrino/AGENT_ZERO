### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain what idempotency means for an API request, and why it matters when a client retries a call. Answer from your training knowledge.

Idempotency means that making the same request multiple times produces the same effect as making it once. A client retrying a call — because of a network timeout, a server crash, or an ambiguous response — must be safe to repeat without causing duplicate state changes. Without idempotency, retries can create duplicate orders, double charges, or duplicate database rows. This is why GET, PUT, and DELETE are idempotent in REST, and why POST is not (it creates resources). For retries, the pattern is to use idempotency keys — the client sends a unique identifier, and the server deduplicates on the first successful write.

### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from repeatedly rewriting the same data as it moves through the tree's levels. Each new write lands in MemTable, flushes to L0 as an SSTable, then gets merged down through L1, L2, L3... in compaction. A single key-value pair might be written to disk 10-50 times before it stabilizes at its final level.

The practical cost is threefold. First, it steals write throughput — your SSD's claimed 500 MB/s sequential write speed becomes maybe 50 MB/s of useful ingest because 90% of those bytes are compaction overhead, not user data. Second, it burns SSD endurance. Consumer drives rated for 150 TBW can fail in months under heavy LSM write amplification, especially with small, random workloads. Third, it creates latency spikes — when compaction kicks off during peak load, foreground writes stall waiting for IO, pushing P99 latencies from single-digit milliseconds into hundreds.

The fundamental tension is that LSM-trees optimize for write throughput on ingestion by batching, but the compaction process that keeps reads fast re-introduces write overhead. LevelDB's default of 10x size ratio between levels means each byte moves roughly N times where N is number of levels — that's the baseline amplification floor before you even account for overlapping L0 SSTables or tombstones.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each reader a point-in-time snapshot of the database as of when the transaction began. Writes create new row versions rather than modifying existing ones in place — the reader keeps showing the old version until the writer commits, and even then the reader holds its snapshot until it finishes. No reader ever waits for a writer because they operate on different versions.

The garbage is **dead row versions** (bloat) — old versions that are no longer visible to any active transaction. They accumulate in pages and must be reclaimed, typically by `VACUUM` or equivalent page-level garbage collection, otherwise index scans and sequential scans pay increasing overhead to skip them, and page density falls.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business capability.

The main risks:

1. **Network fallacies in practice** — services that worked fine in-process now fail intermittently because of latency, partial failure, or timeouts. Retry storms cascade. Circuit breakers and bulkheads are afterthoughts, not design primitives.

2. **Data consistency breaks** — the monolith's single ACID transaction becomes distributed. Teams reach for sagas or eventual consistency, but discover compensating transactions are hard to get right, and stale reads corrupt business logic that assumed atomicity.

3. **Boundary errors** — teams split by "seams" that felt clean on a whiteboard but leak domain logic across service calls. What was a single transaction becomes three round-trips, and the coupling didn't disappear — it just moved to the network.

4. **Observability gap** — the monolith had one log file and one stack trace. Now a request traverses six services, each with its own logging format, and nobody instrumented distributed tracing until production was already on fire.

5. **Team coordination tax** — independence was the goal, but shared schemas, API contracts, and deployment coordination consume more time than the monolith ever did. The cognitive load shifts from code complexity to orchestration complexity.

6. **Premature extraction** — teams pull out services before understanding the domain, then spend months untangling circular dependencies that didn't exist until they were split. The worst case: a distributed big ball of mud that's harder to change than the original monolith.
