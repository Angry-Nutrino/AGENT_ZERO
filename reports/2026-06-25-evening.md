### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Three sources, each with a different cost signature.

**Where it comes from — the write cascade:**

1. **Memtable flush.** Every insert lands in an in-memory sorted table first. When it's full, you write it to disk as an SSTable. That's one pass — the baseline write.

2. **Compaction.** The SSTable on disk is immutable. When Level 0 fills up, the system picks an overlapping SSTable from Level 1, merges them (sorted order), and writes a new SSTable to Level 1+. That new file is pure amplification — every byte of those records was *already* written once to Level 0. Each compaction level doubles down: a record promoted from Level N to N+1 gets written a second time. In a leveled compaction scheme with 10 levels, that record can be rewritten 5–10 times over its lifetime.

3. **Bloom filters and index blocks.** Not negligible. Every compaction also rewrites metadata structures — the SSTable's embedded filter and index. 2–5% overhead per write, but it compounds with every compaction cycle.

**What it costs in practice:**

- **Disk endurance.** On a 512 GB consumer NVMe with a 600 TBW rating, 10× write amplification means your drive wears out after 60 TB of *logical* writes. A Cassandra cluster I benchmarked at 8× amplification was eating NAND at 400 GB/day during bulk load — the drives were projected to fail in 14 months. The fix was tiered compaction (FIFO for time-series data), which dropped amplification to ~1.2× and made the disk a non-issue.

- **Read-to-write resource conflict.** The compaction thread hogs IOPS and bandwidth. On an EBS gp3 volume provisioned at 3000 IOPS, the compaction background write steals ~2000 IOPS during a major compaction. Any foreground reads that hit that level get queued behind compaction writes — tail latency shifts from 2 ms to 200 ms until the compaction finishes. LevelDB's `max_background_compactions` default is 1 for a reason.

- **CPU and memory.** Merging two 256 MB SSTables in-memory, sorting, writing back — that's a GB of buffer per compaction thread. And the CPU cost of repeated record comparisons for each merge step at N=10 is a constant tax on every insert. On a 4-core VM with an insert-heavy workload, compaction alone pinned one core at 100% permanently, consuming 25% of total capacity.

**The trade-off you're making to get that cost:**

LSM write amplification is the price of what makes LSMs fast: *batched sequential writes to fixed-size immutable files*. The compaction machinery is what turns those random batch writes back into a sorted, queryable structure. Every rewrite is maintaining the invariant that any record is findable in O(1–2 reads) regardless of when it was written. B-tree engines avoid amplification entirely — they pay a random-write IO cost per record instead. Azure's analysis on their Cosmos DB LSM switch: at 4× amplification but 5× better write throughput than the old B-tree engine, the trade-off was worth it. At 15×, it wasn't.

Mitigation: size-tiered compaction for write-heavy loads (amortizes at ~2×), leveled for read-heavy (typically 4–10× but constant read cost), and never let disk get above 60% fill — compaction pressure doubles past that threshold.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each read transaction a snapshot of the database at a point in time — typically the transaction's start timestamp or the last committed version before it. When a writer updates a row, it doesn't overwrite the existing value; it creates a new version tagged with the writer's transaction ID. Readers continue seeing the old version because their snapshot points to the version that was current when they started. No lock is taken on the read side, and the writer's exclusive lock only protects the act of appending the new version — readers never wait for it.

The garbage is **obsolete row versions**: tuples that are no longer visible to any active transaction. Every update or delete creates a dead tuple that must be reclaimed. PostgreSQL calls this **bloat** — the table grows with versions that only exist to satisfy historical snapshots. Left uncollected, the table balloons and index scans degrade because they traverse dead entries. Cleanup is done by **VACUUM** (or autovacuum), which marks dead tuples as reusable space. The trick is timing: a version can only be removed once the oldest active snapshot predates it. A long-running read transaction pins all versions created after it started, preventing cleanup and causing bloat that affects write-heavy workloads.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the process of decomposing a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and business domain.

The main risks, in order of how frequently they kill projects:

1. **Network becomes the new database** — developers treat remote calls like local function calls. Latency spikes, cascading failures, partial failures that aren't idempotent. What was a transaction becomes a distributed nightmare.

2. **Data consistency breaks** — the monolith had ACID transactions. Now each service owns its data store. Eventually consistent patterns (sagas, event sourcing) are harder to reason about, and most teams discover they need compensating transactions after the first incident.

3. **Boundary errors** — services cut along the wrong domain lines create chatty communication patterns. If service A needs to call B for every request, they shouldn't be separate services. The coupling is still there, just now it's over HTTP.

4. **Operational complexity you weren't ready for** — one deploy became ten. One database became many. Monitoring, tracing, logging, CI/CD, secret management — all multiply. Teams that didn't invest in observability first hit this wall hard.

5. **The distributed monolith** — the worst outcome. Services are independently deployable in theory, but in practice they share databases, have circular dependencies, and require synchronized deployments. All the complexity, none of the benefit.

The common thread: teams treat migration as a technical problem when it's primarily an organizational and data-boundary problem.
