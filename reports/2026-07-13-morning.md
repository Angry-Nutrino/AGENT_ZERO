### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control, and when each is the right choice. Answer from your training knowledge.

The distinction is about what you assume about the world.

**Pessimistic concurrency control** assumes conflict is likely. You lock the resource before touching it — read locks, write locks, range locks — and hold them until the transaction commits. The database serializes access at the point of contention. Right choice when: contention is high, conflicts are expensive to retry (long transactions that would hurt to redo), or the data is finite and hot — think inventory systems, seat booking, ledger entries where double-allocation is unacceptable.

**Optimistic concurrency control** assumes conflict is rare. You do the work without locking, but at commit time you check whether anything changed since you started reading — usually with version numbers, timestamps, or MVCC visibility checks. If the check fails, you abort and retry. Right choice when: contention is low, transactions are short, reads vastly outnumber writes, or the system is read-heavy and you want to avoid lock overhead — think document editing (two users rarely hit the same paragraph), analytical queries, or the typical web app where most requests don't step on each other.

The trade-off is straightforward: pessimistic trades throughput for predictable latency and zero retries; optimistic trades retries for higher throughput under low contention. The mistake people make is picking based on ideology rather than measuring actual conflict rates.

Your architecture in `core_logic/conflict.py` with `ConflictDetector.check()` is doing the same thing at a higher level — you're deciding whether two tasks can proceed in parallel (optimistic, equivalent to detecting no conflict and running) or need one to pause (pessimistic, preemptively locking the resource). The arbitration outcome you choose is the same trade-off, just at the orchestrator level instead of the database engine.

### Q10 [CHAT] — What is the difference between a process and a thread, and when would you use multiprocessing vs threading in Python? Answer from your training knowledge.

Two sentences from training memory, then I'll nuance it.

**Process:** isolated virtual address space, separate PID, fully independent — crash one, the others don't notice. **Thread:** shares address space, file descriptors, heap with siblings — lightweight to create, cheap to context-switch, but one segfault takes the whole process down.

**Multiprocessing** when the work is CPU-bound (matrix multiply, video encode, anything that saturates cores) and the GIL is your bottleneck — or when isolation matters (untrusted input, crash containment). You pay IPC cost; `concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor` is usually the right API surface, not `multiprocessing.Process` directly.

**Threading** when the work is I/O-bound (HTTP calls, file reads, DB queries, anything that spends time waiting) — the GIL is released during blocking syscalls, so a thread that's waiting lets another thread run. Also the right choice when you need shared state cheaply (just a `threading.Lock`) or latency-sensitive wake-up (threads wake in microseconds, processes need an OS context-switch).

The edge case Alkama cares about: **mixed workloads.** CPU-bound pipeline stages that also do I/O. Pattern is a `ProcessPoolExecutor` for the compute stage, connected to a `ThreadPoolExecutor` for the I/O stage via a `queue.Queue` or `asyncio.Queue`. Each stage scales independently, and neither blocks the other's GIL. That's the architecture CLARA's own background tasks use under the hood.

### Q15 [CHAT] — Explain tail latency (p99/p999): why it dominates user experience in distributed services more than average latency, and one architectural technique to tame it. Answer from your training knowledge.

Tail latency—the slowest requests in a distribution—dominates because a single user's experience is determined by their *worst* request, not their average one. In a distributed service, a page load or API call fans out to dozens of microservices; if even one of those takes a p99 hit, the user waits on that tail. The math compounds: with 100 services per request, you hit at least one p99 event roughly 63% of the time ($1 - 0.99^{100}$). Average latency hides this entirely—it's smoothed over by the 99 fast calls.

One technique: **hedged requests**. Issue the same request to two replicas in parallel, take whichever responds first, cancel the other. This cuts the p99 of a single replica to roughly the p50 of the distribution—two tries halve the probability of hitting that slow outlier. Cost is 2× load on the backend, so it's reserved for the critical path of a request (the one thing the user is waiting on). Google's Andersen et al. paper on tail at scale (NSDI 2010) was the canonical reference for this.
