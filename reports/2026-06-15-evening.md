### Q05 [CHAT] — Explain write amplification in LSM-tree storage engines: where it comes from, and what it costs in practice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Write amplification in LSM-trees comes from the fundamental design tension: the system writes the same data multiple times across different stages of the tree. Each insertion goes to the in-memory memtable, which is flushed to an SSTable in Level 0. Then compaction rewrites and merges that SSTable into lower levels — each level a given piece of data may be read, decompressed, re-sorted, and re-written one or more times before it reaches the deepest level and stabilizes.

The sources break down into three:

1. **Flush amplification** — the memtable flush writes data once to L0.
2. **Compaction amplification** — a given SSTable is rewritten as it merges down through levels. In Leveled compaction (RocksDB default), each piece of data passes through ~10–12 compactions on average from L0 to Lmax. In Size-Tiered (Cassandra default), it's less per-write but spikey — a single compaction can rewrite 4x the data volume.
3. **Bloom-filter false-positives** — minor, but a non-trivial fraction of reads that pass the filter but miss the key still trigger an I/O, which doesn't amplify writes but inflates read-IO relative to the actual data size.

Costs in practice are measurable and real:

- **Throughput ceiling** — LSM write throughput is limited by compaction throughput, not raw disk bandwidth. A system writing 100 MB/s might allocate 30–40 MB/s to background compaction alone, leaving 60–70 MB/s for incoming writes before compaction falls behind and stalls.
- **Write stall latency** — once the compaction queue backs up (usually because L0 fills before compaction catches up), incoming writes are paused entirely. This produces latency spikes from single-digit milliseconds to seconds or tens of seconds. RocksDB's `stall_conditions` counters are the first sign this is happening.
- **SSD endurance** — write amplification of 10x means every logical GB written consumes 10 GB of NAND program/erase cycles. On a consumer SSD rated for 300 TBW, that 10x amplification turns 30 TB of logical writes into an end-of-life scenario. In practice, well-tuned LSM engines on SSDs run between 2x and 5x amplification for workloads with reasonable key sizes.
- **CPU cost** — compaction is not free. Merge-sorting, decompressing-recompressing, checksum verification all burn CPU. A compaction-bound LSM can saturate cores while the disk is underutilized.

The classic trade-off: less aggressive compaction → lower write amplification but worse read performance (more files to check per read). More aggressive → better reads, worse write amplification, higher stall risk. Tuning parameters like `level_compaction_dynamic_level_bytes`, `max_bytes_for_level_multiplier`, and compaction concurrency (`max_background_compactions`) are the levers, but each knob moves the cost somewhere else.

### Q08 [CHAT] — Explain how MVCC (multi-version concurrency control) lets database readers avoid blocking writers, and what kind of garbage it creates that must later be cleaned up. Answer from your training knowledge.

MVCC gives each reader a consistent snapshot by keeping the committed version of every row at the time the reader's transaction began — readers see that frozen state regardless of concurrent writes. Writers create new row versions; they don't overwrite or lock the existing one. Since the reader never waits on a lock and never blocks the writer's new version, the two paths fully decouple.

The garbage is dead row versions — versions that are no longer visible to any active or future transaction. These accumulate in the table storage (heap / index) until a vacuum / garbage-collection process marks them reclaimable and defragments the space. In PostgreSQL that's `VACUUM`; in InnoDB it's the purge thread. If cleanup falls behind, you get table bloat, index bloat, and steadily degrading cache efficiency because the working set is polluted with tombstone data.

### Q15 [CHAT] — What is a microservices migration, and what are the main risks when splitting a monolith into independent services? Answer from your training knowledge.

A microservices migration is the decomposition of a monolithic application into independently deployable services that communicate over a network, each owning its own data and domain boundary.

The main risks:

1. **Network as a new primitive** — what was an in-process function call becomes a remote call subject to latency, partial failure, and timeouts. Retry storms, cascading failures, and the need for circuit breakers and bulkheads follow immediately.

2. **Data consistency** — ACID transactions across services are gone. You now reason in terms of eventual consistency, sagas, and compensating transactions. The distributed transaction that worked in a monolith becomes a distributed coordination problem.

3. **Boundary errors** — the most common failure. Teams split by technical layer (frontend, backend, database) instead of business capability. You end up with distributed monoliths: services that are independently deployed but cannot be changed independently because every operation needs a coordinated deploy across multiple services.

4. **Observability debt** — monoliths have a single log stream, a single process to attach a debugger to, a single point to measure latency. A distributed system requires structured logging with correlation IDs, distributed tracing, metrics aggregation, and a whole new class of dashboards before you can answer "is it slow or is it down" across any flow.

5. **Operational surface area** — one deployment pipeline becomes N. One database becomes N. Monitoring one process becomes monitoring N processes, N hosts or containers, N network paths. The migration often reveals that the organization was not ready to operate the infrastructure it just built.

6. **The strangler fig pattern reversed** — teams extract services by carving out chunks of the monolith, but if the monolith and the new services all share the same database, you got the operational cost of microservices with none of the independence. The monolith must be split at the storage layer, which is the hardest cut to make.
