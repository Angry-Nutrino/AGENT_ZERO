### Q01 [CHAT] — Explain the difference between optimistic and pessimistic concurrency control, and when each is the right choice. Answer from your training knowledge.

Two different bets on whether someone else is going to touch the same data.

**Pessimistic locking** — lock the row/page/table before you touch it. No one else can modify it until you release the lock. You pay the overhead of acquiring and holding the lock on every operation, but you never retry. Right choice when contention is high (same records, many writers), when writes are long or complex, or when a retry would be expensive or user-visible (financial transactions, inventory deduction at checkout).

**Optimistic concurrency** — read without locking, write with a version check. If the version changed between read and write, the write fails and you retry from scratch. Right choice when contention is low (most operations hit different records), when reads dominate writes, or when lock overhead would dwarf the occasional retry cost. Also preferred in distributed systems where locks are hard to coordinate.

The threshold: if your conflict rate exceeds roughly 5–10% of writes, optimistic starts to degrade — retries accumulate latency and compound under load. Below that, it wins on throughput and scalability.

Your analytics ingestion pipeline — a few billion rows per month with a batch-oriented write pattern — is an optimistic concurrency case. Low odds of two batches touching the same record's version. Pessimistic locking there would add coordination overhead for no benefit.

### Q10 [CHAT] — What is the difference between symmetric and asymmetric encryption? When is each typically used? Answer from your training knowledge.

Symmetric encryption uses a single shared key for both encryption and decryption. Asymmetric uses a public/private key pair — the public key encrypts, the private decrypts, and vice versa for signing.

Symmetric is used for bulk data encryption where both sides already share a key — disk encryption, TLS session keys after the handshake, file encryption. It's fast and efficient at scale. Asymmetric is used for key exchange, authentication, and digital signatures — the initial TLS handshake, SSH, PGP, certificate signing. It handles the trust problem that symmetric can't solve alone.

Real systems don't choose one — they combine them. Asymmetric solves the key distribution problem, symmetric handles the actual data.

### Q15 [CHAT] — Explain how Python's asyncio event loop runs many coroutines on a single thread, and what happens to the whole loop if one coroutine performs a long blocking CPU-bound computation. Answer from your training knowledge.

Single-threaded concurrency via an event loop: `await` yields control back to the loop, which then schedules the next ready coroutine from its runnable queue. No parallelism — cooperative multitasking on one thread.

A long CPU-bound coroutine that never hits `await` blocks the entire loop. Every other coroutine starves — no socket data is read, no timers fire, no responses are sent — until that computation finishes. The fix is `loop.run_in_executor(None, blocking_func)` to offload it to a thread pool, freeing the loop to continue scheduling other work.
